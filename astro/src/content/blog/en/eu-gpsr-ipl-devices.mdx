**Short answer:** since 13 December 2024 a home IPL device can only be placed on the EU market if an operator established inside the EU is named as the responsible person, with a postal and electronic address, on the product or its packaging. A factory outside the EU cannot be that operator. What the factory can do is hand the seller the data pack that makes the appointment work.

## The date, and why it keeps coming up

13 December 2024 is when Regulation (EU) 2023/988 took over from the General Product Safety Directive that had been in place since 2001. Nearly two years on, the practical effect has less to do with customs than with paperwork arriving at the wrong moment.

Here is the pattern. A brand owner gets a listing live, orders the first production run, and then a marketplace review or a distributor's compliance questionnaire asks one question: who is the EU responsible person for this product, and where is the address on the packaging? If the answer is a Chinese factory address, the listing stops. Not because the device is unsafe. Because the label names nobody who can be held responsible inside the EU.

In our experience the paperwork stage stops more shipments than the technical stage does.

## The responsible person is your buyer's legal duty, and your documentation duty

Under the regulation, the operator named on the product must be established in the EU. For a manufacturer outside the EU, the route is a written mandate appointing an importer, an authorised representative, or a fulfilment service provider.

That appointment is signed by your customer. The factory cannot sign it, cannot be named in it, and should never let its own address be printed where the responsible person's address belongs. What the factory controls is whether the appointment can be completed at all.

### What the written mandate needs before anyone signs

An EU operator will not accept the role on a promise. Expect them to ask for the technical file, a risk analysis, the declaration of conformity with the standards actually applied, and evidence that units can be traced back to a batch. If those four things do not exist, the mandate does not get signed, and the order does not ship.

## The data pack: six items, and who produces each

| Item | What it contains | Who produces it | Why the EU operator needs it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Technical file | Design and construction information, schematics, component list, photos of markings | Factory | Kept for ten years; produced on request to market surveillance |
| Risk analysis | Hazards identified for intended use and foreseeable misuse, with the measures taken | Factory | The regulation asks for an internal risk analysis, not a generic safety statement |
| Declaration of conformity | Regulation and standards applied, model identification, signatory | Factory or the legal manufacturer named on the DoC | Must match the standards in the test reports, and must match the model on the carton |
| Test report list | Which standards were tested, by which body, on which model, with dates | Factory | The operator needs to know what is covered, and what is not |
| Traceability coding | Batch or serial number, and where it sits on the unit and the carton | Factory | Without it, a recall cannot be scoped |
| Label artwork | The full label block, including the EU operator's name and address | Factory drafts, operator confirms | The address that goes on the box belongs to the operator, not the factory |

Two of those items are the ones that most often arrive late. The risk analysis, because factories tend to treat it as a formality, and the label artwork, because the EU operator's details are only known at the end of the project. Both can be drafted early with placeholders. Neither can be improvised at the port.

## Two doors, and the factory only gets to walk through one

Home IPL devices reach the EU by one of two routes, and the paperwork is different for each. The choice belongs to the brand, not to the factory, but the factory has to say clearly which route it can support.

| | Consumer electrical route | Medical device route |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | Low Voltage Directive, EMC Directive, RoHS, with a Declaration of Conformity | MDR 2017/745; home IPL sits under Annex XVI, class IIa |
| Who assesses | Self-declaration, backed by test reports from an accredited lab | Notified body, with a certificate issued to a named manufacturer |
| GPSR | Applies, since no sector legislation covers general product safety for these risks | Applies only where MDR does not cover the same risks |
| Typical timeline | Weeks, once the design is frozen | Months, and the certificate is held by one legal entity only |
| What the buyer must arrange | EU responsible person, plus importer details on the label | Everything above, plus the certificate holder relationship |

We wrote separately about [which route applies to which device and why the MDR certificate question is usually aimed at the wrong party](/blog/eu-mdr-home-ipl-devices). Worth reading before a European buyer asks you for an MDR certificate on a device you place as consumer electronics.

## Four mismatches that stop shipments

Not safety failures. Paperwork that does not agree with itself.

**The DoC names a standard the report does not cover.** A declaration citing an older edition of a standard while the report tests the current one is the most common version of this. Customs rarely reads it. The EU operator's compliance consultant does, and rejects it.

**The model number on the certificate and the carton disagree.** Your product family has a platform name, a model code, and a customer SKU. If the DoC says one and the master carton says another, the responsible person cannot connect the shipment to the file. The fix is a single model identification table agreed with the buyer before the artwork is approved.

**No batch code, so a recall cannot be scoped.** Traceability is not paperwork for its own sake. If a batch is questioned, the operator has to know which units came from it. A date code printed on the inner box, referenced in the packing list, is enough. Nothing at all is not.

**The label names the factory where the responsible person's address belongs.** This happens when a factory template has its own address in the manufacturer block and the customer's EU address gets added next to it, in the wrong field. The label then states two parties, and the operator is not one of them.

## What the label block looks like when it is right

```
Manufacturer:      <legal entity, country>
EU responsible:    <operator name>, <postal address>, <email>
Model:             <model code as used in the DoC>
Batch / date code: <code as used in the packing list>
Electrical rating: <V, Hz, W>
Warnings:          <the wording the operator's market requires>
CE marking:        <placed per the applicable directive>
```

The empty field is the point. The factory can print and pre-verify every line except the second one. That line arrives from the customer, and until it does, the artwork is not final.
