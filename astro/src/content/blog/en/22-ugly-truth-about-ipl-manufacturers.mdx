The IPL hair removal market is growing fast, and more people are jumping in to grab a piece of it.

We've dealt with IPL brands for years — clients from first-time founders to Costco-scale retailers — so we've basically run into every trick in the circle. Some made us angry, some made us laugh, and only a very few genuinely scared us.

This post lays out the most common tricks one by one: how each one deceives, how to see through it, and what to do when you meet it. If you're about to pick a contract manufacturer for your own brand, read this before you sign anything.

## The Three Most Damaging Tricks

The tricks come in many flavors, but the most dangerous ones boil down to three categories.

| Category | What Gets Faked | The Real Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Fluence (J/cm²) and skin sensors | Zero results, or burned customer skin |
| Power adapter | Uncertified adapters passed off as medical-grade | Fire, explosion, electric shock |
| Certification | FDA 510(k), CE, ISO 13485 | Lawsuits, banned accounts, fines |

Let's go through them one by one.

## Energy Fake #1: Just Make It Glow

A buyer from the Middle East sent us a photo of a Philips hair removal device and asked whether we could produce it. We looked at it — the machine was an exact match for a certain Philips model, a replica built from it. We told him no: we don't copy other brands.

I still remember his answer. He said: "I don't need the same energy. I just need it to look exactly the same and glow when I press the button."

That still makes me angry when I think about it. He didn't care whether the light could actually remove hair. He didn't care whether the skin sensor worked well, or whether the sensor even existed — he never asked. What he wanted was a flashlight that looked like a Philips hair removal device.

Why did he ask for this? Because he had already imported similar fakes from another supplier and they sold decently, for a while. Customers received a beautiful box, the light came on, and it did absolutely nothing for their body hair.

Even worse was what happened to his previous supplier. That was a small workshop that specialized in unauthorized Philips replicas. Philips' legal team came knocking — a cease-and-desist letter plus a lawsuit threat — and the workshop shut down overnight.

So this buyer was in a hurry to find a new supplier, and his standard was simple: reproduce the fake exactly as it was. Whoever takes that order is asking for trouble.

Any buyer who asks only for looks and ignores performance entirely is an order you should not take. A brand like that won't last; when it collapses, your reputation may go down with it.

## Energy Fake #2: Numbers Written on the Screen

The second trick is more sophisticated — the lying happens in the numbers.

The device actually outputs 10 J/cm². That's already high, and without cooling it's even a bit dangerous. The supplier's approach is to change the display software so the machine shows 18 J/cm² when tested, then write "max energy 20 J/cm²" on the spec sheet and product page.

From a distance, the numbers are indeed intimidating. 20 J/cm² sounds far stronger than the 5–6 J/cm² reputable brands typically use, and consumers who don't know the physics easily assume more energy means better results.

But that number can't stand up, for three hard reasons.

- An uncooled IPL device delivering 20 J/cm² would cause second-degree burns on most skin types.
- Most home IPL power supplies simply can't feed 20 J/cm² — the capacitors can't store that much energy.
- 20 J/cm² demands enormous heat dissipation, which a cheap plastic housing can't provide.

So these numbers live only on paper and on screens. In real use, either the results fall far short or it directly injures people.

To see through this trick, three steps.

1. Ask for an independent third-party test report, not one the supplier produced themselves.
2. Verify the energy measurement method — a calibrated energy meter; software readouts don't count.
3. Cross-check the claimed energy against the power supply capacity — basic physics can't lie.

## Power Adapters: Bombs You Plug Into the Wall

Next up: the power adapter. Most brand owners never think about this thing — and that's exactly what dishonest suppliers count on.

Adapters in the industry come in three grades.

| Grade | Description | Safety Level | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medical grade | Passed IEC 60601 certification (medical electrical equipment safety standard) | Very high | Premium brands, e.g., RoseSkinCo |
| Commercially certified | Not medical-grade, but UL/CE/FCC/ROHS certified | High | Most legitimate mid-tier brands |
| Uncertified | No certification, testing cut wherever possible, cheapest components | Very low, dangerous | Scam products |

The danger is in the third tier. An uncertified adapter costs $2–3 apiece; a certified one costs $8–12. What the price difference cuts out is overcurrent protection, thermal shutdown, and proper insulation.

What can happen?

- The adapter overheats and the housing melts.
- Internal components short-circuit and start a small fire.
- Capacitors burst and the adapter blows apart.
- The user gets an electric shock.

Any one of these four, landing on your brand, is more than you can absorb. You could sell 10,000 units without a single incident, and it still won't save you from one TikTok video of smoke and flames. That video will be the only image anyone remembers of you.

To hold this front, three rules.

1. Put "medical-grade IEC 60601 adapter" in the contract, and spot-test units on arrival.
2. Ask for the certification documents, then verify them with the issuing body.
3. If a supplier offers to "save $5 per unit" by swapping in a cheaper adapter, refuse outright and switch suppliers.

## Certificate Fraud: Even FDA Documents Can Be Forged

More vicious than energy and adapters is the third category: certificate fraud.

The fake documents we've seen fall into roughly three types.

- Fake FDA 510(k) clearance letters. Made in Photoshop, with numbers copied from other products' genuine clearances.
- Fake supplier cooperation agreements. The document claims a partnership with a certified factory — a factory that doesn't exist.
- Mismatched model numbers. The contract lists Model X, the Amazon listing shows Model Y.

The scale of the problem is bigger than most people think. By some estimates, more than 90% of IPL hair removal devices sold on Amazon US use fake or misleading FDA documentation.

Why does it slip through? Amazon's first-round document review is automated. A PDF forged well enough can get through — for a while.

But people do come checking later. Amazon has dedicated teams watching product safety compliance, and when they find a problem, a whole process runs.

- The listing is removed.
- The seller account is suspended.
- Money in the account is frozen.
- In serious cases, Amazon refers the seller for criminal prosecution.

We've seen brands doing $6 million a year on Amazon, built entirely on fake FDA documents. When Amazon came to audit, the whole business collapsed, lawsuits followed, and the founders now face possible jail time.

In the US, IPL is a Class II medical device. Selling an uncertified medical device with forged clearance documents constitutes wire fraud and counterfeiting — both felonies.

Verification is easy: the FDA's 510(k) database is public, and you can check it yourself. First, understand the difference between two words. A device that has received 510(k) clearance is officially called "cleared" by the FDA. "Approved" refers only to products that went through the stricter PMA pathway — the [FDA's definition of 510(k)](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/premarket-submissions-selecting-and-preparing-correct-submission/premarket-notification-510k) says this clearly. If any seller boasts that their IPL device is "FDA approved," start with a question mark.

1. Open the FDA 510(k) database (https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm)
2. Search by applicant name or product name.
3. Confirm the model actually appears in the database and the model number matches.
4. If the supplier says "we have FDA clearance," ask for the 510(k) number and check it yourself.

If the supplier refuses or makes excuses, walk away.

## Where These Lessons Came From

None of the traps above are in any textbook — every one of them was taught to us by clients. Some clients had already been burned once by the time they came to us.

The Middle Eastern buyer who wanted a glowing replica was a wake-up call. After that, we understood something: quite a few brands in this market are selling glow-up toys with pretty boxes.

The inflated-energy supplier was exposed by a client's test report. The client sent the product to a third-party lab and it failed. The screen showed 18 J/cm²; the calibrated energy meter measured 9 J/cm² — half the claimed number.

The fake FDA document: a client excitedly showed us their "clearance letter." We searched the FDA database — the number belonged to another company, another product, and had been cleared ten years earlier.

Every one of these was infuriating at the time, but in hindsight each taught us where to watch closely, what must be asked, and which orders we must never touch.

Today our company has zero tolerance for these things. A buyer asking for fake replicas — we don't take it. A supplier inflating specs — we don't negotiate. Certification documents that can't be verified — the deal is dead.

## Run Through This Checklist Before You Sign

Before the contract is signed and the money is paid, go through every item below.

| Check | Action |
|---|---|
| Energy verification | Require a test report from an independent lab; the supplier's own test doesn't count. |
| Adapter certification | Put medical grade (IEC 60601) or commercial certification in the contract; ask for certificate copies. |
| FDA 510(k) | Search the public database and confirm the model number matches. |
| CE mark | Verify the notified body number; not every CE mark is real. |
| ISO 13485 | Check the certificate-issuing body; only accredited registrars count. |
| Factory visit or video | Look at the production line and ask to see the test equipment. |
| Sample testing | Order samples, test the energy output yourself, and use the device for a few weeks. |

If the supplier refuses any single item above, stop. The money you save by skipping verification is far less than the damage once something goes wrong.
