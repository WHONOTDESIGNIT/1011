**Short answer:** the hardware knowledge transfers and the claims do not. UV-C sells disinfection with a dose against a surface; IPL sells hair removal with an energy density against skin. A germ claim on an IPL device is unsupported in every market we ship to.

## The same word, two different products

"Light-based device" covers a UV-C steriliser, an LED nail lamp, a red light panel and an IPL handset. They share a power supply and nothing else that matters.

| | UV-C | IPL |
|---|---|---|
| Target | Microbes on a surface or in air | Hair follicle under skin |
| Claim basis | Log reduction at a defined dose | Hair reduction over a treatment course |
| Safety frame | Eye and skin exposure limits | Photobiological safety of the lamp |
| Typical regulation | Biocide or electrical | Electrical device, market-specific |

A factory that has built UV-C products has real advantages in power electronics, thermal design and housing. None of them include a germ claim.

## Where the crossover actually pays

- **Optical design.** Filters, reflectors and window materials are shared problems.
- **Thermal management.** Both products run a light source hard and need to shed heat.
- **Manufacturing discipline.** Ingress protection, drop tolerance, and label durability.
- **Supplier base.** Lamp, quartz and driver suppliers overlap.

That is a genuine head start. It is also a head start on hardware, not on the file that lets you sell it.

## The three claims to delete

If a hygiene company writes IPL copy, three phrases appear and all three have to go:

1. Any germ, bacteria or sterilising claim.
2. Any "safe for all skin" or "no side effects" line.
3. Any medical claim, including "treats" or "cures".

Replace them with what the test report supports: the energy density, the window area, the pulse duration, the cooling, and the contraindications.

## What a buyer will ask you

The first question from a distributor is not about the light source. It is "what is the file", meaning the test reports and the declaration that the device meets the rules of the destination market.

Answer that before you answer the wavelength question.

## What you will have to justify to your own product team

If your company builds UV-C products and is considering an IPL line, the internal argument will be about the light source. The real argument is about the claim.

Bring the three claims you cannot make, and the evidence basis for each refusal. A germ claim needs a biocide route. A "safe for all skin" line needs a population that does not exist. A medical claim needs a device classification you do not have.

Then bring what you can say: energy density, window area, pulse duration, cooling, contraindications. Every one of those comes from a test report, which means every one of them survives a distributor's question.

## The cost of getting this wrong

A claim that overreaches does not fail slowly. It fails at the point of retail, when a buyer's compliance team reads the pack, and the whole range comes off the shelf while the copy is rewritten.

That is the number to put in front of your product team: not the fine, the lost listing. Compare the revenue of a season against the cost of a relabel and the argument ends quickly.

## The first order, if you get that far

Buy one unit and read its packaging against your own compliance thinking. The exercise is not to find fault, it is to see how much of the file you would have to rebuild to sell it confidently.

Then compare the manual language with the market list. If the device ships with one language and you sell in four, the gap is your first project rather than the factory's.

A hygiene company entering this category usually discovers the same thing first: the hardware is easy to source and the documentation is the work.
