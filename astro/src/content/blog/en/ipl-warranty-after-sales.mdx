**Short answer:** warranty cost is failure rate times replacement cost, plus logistics, plus the support time before every return. Brands that do not model it discover it in year two, when the installed base is big enough for the rate to matter.

## The line item nobody prices

A warranty is usually written by marketing and paid for by operations. "Two years" reads as confidence on the box and as an unmodelled liability on the P&L.

The arithmetic is not complicated:

| Component | Typical share of field failures | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Lamp or flash cartridge | Large — it is the consumable | A cartridge, shipped |
| Capacitor or driver board | Small in count, high in cost | A unit or a board swap |
| Cooling fan or vent | Dust and blockage | A repair or a replacement |
| Battery | Age, not use | A replacement, usually out of warranty |
| User error | Larger than brands admit | Support time, sometimes a free unit |

The last row is the one that kills margin, because it is unbounded and it is not in any reliability test.

## Repair or replace is a decision, not a policy

For a consumer IPL device, a repair usually costs more than the unit's margin: the handset is not designed to be opened, the labour is skilled, and the return shipping is paid twice.

So most brands replace. That is defensible, but it has a consequence that should be priced in: every replacement is a full unit cost, not a part cost. A 3% failure rate on 20,000 units is 600 units given away, and at a 35% margin that is a visible fraction of the year's profit.

The decision to replace also sets the acceptable failure rate. If a replacement costs a full unit, the rate has to stay low; if a cartridge swap solves most cases, a higher rate is survivable. Which means the warranty cost is a design input, not a post-launch surprise.

## The window is longer than the box says

Consumer law in most of the markets we ship to gives a buyer a statutory remedy that runs longer than the manufacturer's stated warranty — often two years from delivery, sometimes more, and the burden of proof shifts toward the seller after the first months.

So a "one-year warranty" is a marketing decision, not the legal limit. A brand that budgets for twelve months and sells into a market with a two-year statutory period has under-priced every unit it sells there.

The fix is not to lengthen the printed warranty. It is to model the statutory window, which is what actually gets claimed.

## The support cost before the return

Most returns begin as a support message, and the message is usually not about the device. It is about not seeing results, using it on the wrong setting, or expecting a clinic outcome at home.

That is a documentation problem with a warranty cost attached. Every unanswered question in the manual becomes a ticket, and a ticket that ends in a return costs the unit plus the shipping plus the time.

The cheapest warranty improvement is a manual that answers the questions the support inbox actually receives. If a brand does not read its own tickets before writing the next manual, it is paying for the same failure every quarter.

## What a buyer should ask a manufacturer

- **What is the field failure rate, by component?** Not the lab rate. Field.
- **What is the replacement policy for a failed unit?** Unit swap, board swap or repair, and who pays freight.
- **Which certificates cover the market's statutory window?** Certification and consumer law are different questions.
- **Is there a spare-parts path for year three?** A device is only as good as its consumable supply.

## What you will have to justify internally

The question will be why the warranty reserve is as large as it is.

Bring the failure rate by component, the replacement cost per failure, and the statutory window in each market you sell into. A reserve built on those three numbers survives a finance review. A reserve built on "we assume 2%" does not, and the difference shows up as a surprise in the second year, when the installed base is large enough for the assumption to be tested.
