**Short answer:** home IPL devices circulate in trade under at least two tariff codes, 8510.30 and 8543.70, because neither heading was written with this product in mind. The code decides duty, preferential treatment and reporting, the broker of record makes the call, and the one thing a buyer should insist on is that every document in the shipment agrees with it.

## The two headings, and why both turn up

The first heading covers shavers, hair clippers and hair-removing appliances, and its own wording assumes a self-contained electric motor. That fits a shaver. An IPL device has no motor at all: a xenon lamp, a capacitor bank, a control board and a cooling system.

That mismatch is why the residual heading appears in the same trade: electrical machines and apparatus with an individual function, not specified or included elsewhere. It is the honest home for a product the schedule did not anticipate.

Both codes end up in real paperwork. On one order of 504 gradient-housing units, the export declaration carried the hair-removing-appliance code, the product pages carried the same number as a US code, and a second packing list carried the residual code. Two documents, one shipment, two answers.

Nothing about that is unusual in this product category. What is unusual is catching it before the declaration rather than after.

## Why the code is not a paperwork detail

Customs duty is a function of three things: the code, the origin, and the programme the origin qualifies for. Change one and the number in the landed cost moves.

| What the code affects | Why |
|---|---|
| Duty rate | Different headings carry different rates, and the gap is often larger than the freight |
| Preferential treatment | Free trade agreements are negotiated per heading |
| Additional tariff programmes | Origin-based measures are applied by heading, not by product description |
| Import licensing and standards | Some destinations attach market surveillance or registration to specific headings |
| Statistical reporting and quota | The importer's own reporting is filed by code |

So the code question is not administrative tidiness. It is the difference between a landed cost that was calculated and one that was guessed.

## Who actually decides

The broker of record declares, and the declaration is theirs to sign. That is not a formality to route around; it is the reason a broker will refuse to use a code they cannot defend.

Three things follow for a buyer:

- **Ask for the code in writing before the order is placed**, not on the commercial invoice after the goods have sailed.
- **Ask which tariff schedule it is checked against.** A code that is right on the US schedule is not automatically right for the export declaration in the country of origin. Those are two different tariff books, and they are allowed to disagree.
- **Ask what happens if the model changes.** Add a motor for a rotating head, remove the lamp for an LED variant, and the heading can move with it.

A supplier who answers all three has done this before. A supplier who says "we always use one code" has told you they have never been audited.

## The consistency rule

This is the part that catches shipments, and it is entirely within a factory's control: **the commercial invoice, the packing list, the certificate of origin and the declaration all have to carry the same code, and the goods have to match the description behind it.**

A shipment where the invoice says one heading and the declaration says another is a discrepancy at the border. It does not matter which one was right.

The other half is the description. A declared description of "hair removal device" sits comfortably under one heading and awkwardly under the other. Descriptions and codes have to be chosen together.

## What we do on our orders

We keep the code on the commercial documents and reconcile it against the declaration before the broker files, which is how the discrepancy in that 504-unit order was caught rather than discovered at the port. We use the same code on the product pages so a buyer reading the site and a broker reading the invoice are looking at the same number.

What we do not do is tell a customer what their import duty will be. That answer depends on their tariff schedule, their origin rules and their own broker's view, and we are not the party signing the entry.

## What to ask before the first shipment

1. Which code will appear on the commercial invoice, and which schedule was it checked against?
2. Has that code been used on a previous shipment of this exact model, and did it clear without a query?
3. If the model changes, who re-checks the code, and when?
4. Can the invoice, packing list, certificate of origin and declaration be checked against each other before filing?

Four questions, one page of answers, filed with the order. It is cheaper than a reclassification.
