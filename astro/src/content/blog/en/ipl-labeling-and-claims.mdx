A home IPL device is classified by what it claims to do, not by what it contains. Change the promise on the box and the regulatory route can change with it, on identical hardware.

This is uncomfortable for marketing teams, because the strongest copy is usually the copy that makes a claim the compliance file cannot support.

## Why the claim, not the hardware, sets the classification

Regulators classify devices by intended purpose. Intended purpose is established by the manufacturer's own statements: the label, the instructions, the website, the advertising.

So the same IPL platform can be placed on the market as a cosmetic appliance performing hair removal, or as a medical device treating a condition. The hardware did not change. The stated purpose did.

That is not a loophole. It is the mechanism, and it cuts both ways. A brand that ups its claims to win a comparison table may be reclassifying its product without knowing it, and the evidence needed for the new classification does not exist yet.

## The claims that are safe on a home device

Hair removal, described plainly. Statements about the treatment itself: session length, energy level, cooling, number of flashes, skin tone suitability with the correct caveat about darker tones and patch testing.

Statements about the device rather than the body are generally safe. "Nine intensity levels" is a product fact. "Reduces pore size" is a claim about a person.

Comparatives need care. "Faster than the previous model" is a product claim. "Better results than laser" is a clinical claim dressed as a comparison.

## The claims that quietly move you into medical

Skin rejuvenation. Acne treatment. Wrinkle reduction. Pigmentation correction. Improved complexion. Anything that names a condition, or that promises a change to the skin beyond hair.

The pattern is not subtle once you look for it: claims about a condition or about the appearance of skin. Hair is the one target that a cosmetic IPL device is allowed to address.

We have watched brands discover this at the label proof stage, after the artwork was designed, which is the most expensive possible moment. ## Label elements that are not negotiable

The party responsible for the device. Not the factory, unless the factory is that party. This is the same identity question that runs through the whole supply chain, and it decides who answers when something goes wrong.

The model identifier. It has to match the certificate, the technical file and the invoice. A label with a model number that does not appear on the certificate is a label that describes an untested device.

Electrical ratings and the adapter specification. The adapter is part of the device as shipped. A rating label that does not match the adapter in the box is a labelling defect even when both are individually fine.

Warnings, in the language of each market. Including the eye-safety warning and the skin-tone caution.

The conformity marking, where applicable, and only where the basis for it exists.

## Claims across markets, in one table

| Claim | US | EU | UK |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hair removal | Usually fine under a cleared device | Fine as a cosmetic function | Fine, with UKCA where applicable |
| Skin rejuvenation | Raises intended-use questions | Can reclassify as a medical device | Similar to EU |
| Acne treatment | Condition claim, medical territory | Medical device territory | Medical device territory |
| "Safe for all skin types" | Needs the darker-tone caveat | Needs the caveat and patch-test guidance | Same |

The table is a pattern, not advice. The specific market question belongs to a regulatory advisor who has seen the actual label.

## How to rewrite a line without losing the sale

Three substitutions that keep the benefit and drop the claim.

Replace the outcome with the mechanism. "Smoothes skin" becomes "sapphire cooling reduces surface temperature during treatment". The second is a product fact and it still communicates comfort.

Replace the condition with the routine. "Treats acne-prone skin" becomes "designed for weekly sessions on the face and body". You keep the use case, you lose the medical claim.

Replace the comparison with the specification. "Better than laser" becomes "3.3 cm² window and 999,999 flashes". Numbers survive compliance review where adjectives do not.

That last substitution is the same discipline we apply to [product copy generally](/blog/50-private-label-ipl-gudid-registration-and-responsibility), where the labeler of record carries the consequences of whatever the label says.

## Where this goes wrong in a private-label project

The brand writes the copy, the factory prints the box, and nobody checks the claim against the certificate. Six months later a marketplace takes the listing down and the question of who owns the claim becomes a contract argument.

The fix is cheap and early: put the claim list in the technical file, next to the certificate, and make the label artwork a controlled document. Then a new marketing line is a file change with an owner, rather than a proof that goes straight to print.
