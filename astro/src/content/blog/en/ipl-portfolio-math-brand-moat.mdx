The next fight in home IPL is not about joules. It is about how many products a customer buys from you before she stops thinking of you as a device.

Philips and Braun understood this early. They are not device companies that happen to sell IPL; they are portfolio companies that happen to include IPL. Sensica appears to be building the same way, with three pillars and very few SKUs. That combination, not a faster lamp, is what makes a brand hard to displace.

## The single-category trap

KU2 Cosmetics is the cleanest example of the other path. It sells across categories, so a weak quarter in one line does not take the company with it.

The brands that scaled on creator spikes show the opposite. Kenzzi, HappySkinCo and RoseSkinCo all built impressive revenue on influencer placement. When the spend paused, the sales line reverted. The category has watched this pattern repeat often enough that it is no longer a surprise; it is a business model.

There is a structural reason. A single-category brand has one demand curve. Every competitor, every platform change, every creator fee increase lands on that curve directly. A portfolio brand has several curves that do not peak at the same time.

## China's supply base removes the manufacturing moat

Fifteen years ago, a single-category brand could defend itself with supply relationships. That moat is gone.

An entrepreneur in the GCC, Europe or North America can now approach a Chinese IPL hair removal device manufacturer and source a platform that already has certification, tooling and packaging in place. The capital required to enter is lower than at any point in the category's history, which means the number of attackers is not limited by factory access. It is limited by distribution and brand.

That is why the manufacturing advantage is a bad place to stand. If your only edge is that you found a factory, the next founder finds the same factory next quarter.

## The new entrant is a distributor, not a factory

The founder entering this category in 2026 is usually not a manufacturer. She is a distributor or a brand operator in the GCC, Europe or North America who already owns a customer list and a channel.

That changes the competition. She does not need to build a factory, because the factory already exists and will quote her in days. She needs a platform, a certification path and a launch plan, and she can buy all three. Her advantage is distribution, which compounds; a factory advantage does not.

The implication for incumbents is uncomfortable. The threat does not arrive as a cheaper device from a no-name brand. It arrives as a well-run brand with a better channel, using the same supply base you use.

## What multi-category actually looks like

Sensica's structure is worth studying because it is disciplined rather than broad. Three pillars: hair removal, anti-aging, body care. A small number of products in each. The SKU count is deliberately low, and every product shares the same visual and clinical language.

A catalogue brand adds categories to fill a grid. The logic here is minimum viable portfolio: enough products that a customer has a second reason to return, few enough that inventory, certification and creative stay under control.

A customer who buys a device, then an anti-aging product, then a body care product is not buying three things. She is confirming a decision she already made. Each additional category raises switching cost without raising acquisition cost.

## "We sell a lifestyle" is the lazy version

Plenty of founders summarise this as selling a lifestyle. That explanation is too thin to act on.

A lifestyle is a positioning claim. A portfolio is an operating system: shared customers, shared compliance, shared warehouse, shared creative. The first is a sentence in a deck. The second shows up in inventory turns and repeat rate.

The test is simple. If the second product disappeared tomorrow, would the first product's customer notice? If the answer is no, the second product is decoration.

## Reason two: a stable line beats a spiky one

The second argument for the portfolio is financial, and it is easier to see in the numbers.

On the projections we have seen for Sensica, 2027 runs at roughly 5,000 units for the year, with 2028 modelled at around 15,000. Whatever the exact figures turn out to be, the shape is a line that climbs.

Compare that with a creator-led brand that ships 20,000 units in a month and 200 the next. The annual total can look similar. The business underneath does not. A spike forces you to hold inventory, staff for peak, and discount to clear the tail. A line lets you plan production, negotiate with the factory, and keep the price.

This is the argument in [our unit-economics piece](/blog/ipl-brand-unit-economics): cash timing, not demand, is what kills device brands. A spiky sales line is a cash-timing problem wearing a growth costume.

## ROI has a floor and a ceiling

The quantified version of this argument is a band, not a target.

Return on ad spend is revenue divided by media cost. Below 3, the channel is losing money once returns, warranty and fulfilment are counted. That is the floor.

Above 6, the constraint flips. You are not under-investing in growth; you are running into production capacity and working capital. More spend at that point means more inventory, more receivables, more risk. That is the ceiling.

Most operators manage one end of the band. They cut when ROI drops, and they scale when it climbs, without asking whether the supply chain and the balance sheet can absorb the second decision. The band is the discipline: hold the number inside it, and let the portfolio smooth the months when one product is soft.

Media mix matters here as much as the number. A brand that relies on one platform for 70% of spend has an ROI that is really a platform-risk measure. Two channels with correlated audiences behave like one channel during a policy change, which is exactly when the spike and the trough arrive together.

## Reason three: the unit economics pay for the team

The third argument is the one founders underweight, because it is unglamorous.

Take a single flagship at 5,000 units a year and an average selling price near USD 445. That is about USD 2.2 million in revenue from one model. At a 50% gross margin, roughly USD 1.1 million of gross profit. Run it with five people and the arithmetic is about USD 445,000 of revenue per head, and USD 222,500 of gross profit per head.

Those are illustrative assumptions, not audited figures. The point is what they permit. At that productivity, a company can pay above market, keep experienced operators, and fund certification without a funding round. At half that productivity, it cannot. The salary conversation starts with unit economics.

This is why the portfolio matters for hiring as much as for revenue. Three pillars mean one operations team, one compliance function, one warehouse. The fixed cost is shared, so revenue per head rises with every category.

## What a challenger should copy

If you are building a brand in this category, the playbook is narrower than it looks.

Pick two or three adjacent categories and stay there. Hair removal, aftercare, and one adjacent skin or body line is a legitimate portfolio; ten categories is a catalogue. Keep the SKU count low enough that certification and inventory stay legible.

Hold acquisition inside the band. A 3-to-6 ROI target sounds conservative until you price the working capital of a spike.

Build the model before the launch. Lifetime value, payback period, and revenue per head are not finance decoration; they decide whether the second product is fundable.

## FAQ

### Why do single-category IPL brands fail?

They inherit the full volatility of one demand curve. A platform change, a creator fee increase or a competitor's price cut hits the whole business at once, with no second product to absorb it.

### What ROI should an IPL brand target?

Between 3 and 6 on revenue over media cost. Below 3 the channel loses money after returns and fulfilment; above 6 you are limited by production capacity and working capital.

### Is Sensica really a top-three brand?

That is a forecast, not a fact. What is observable is the portfolio structure and the stable sales line, and those are the two things that usually precede scale in this category.
