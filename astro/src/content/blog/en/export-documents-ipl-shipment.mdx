**Short answer:** an IPL shipment moves on five documents, and containers rarely wait because one is missing. They wait because the numbers on them disagree. The five are the commercial invoice, the packing list, the certificate of origin, the transport document and the customs declaration, and a customs officer reads them against each other.

## What each one is for

| Document | What it proves | Who issues it | What it is compared against |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial invoice | What was sold, to whom, at what price and terms | Seller | The declaration and the purchase order |
| Packing list | What is physically in the boxes, weights and dimensions | Seller or factory | The carton labels and the measured weight |
| Certificate of origin | Where the goods were made, for duty and trade measures | Chamber of commerce or approved body | The declaration, and the origin rules of the destination |
| Bill of lading or air waybill | What was handed to the carrier, and where it is going | Carrier | The invoice and the packing list |
| Customs declaration | The tariff classification and the declared elements | Declarant, on the exporter's instructions | All of the above |

Each one answers a different question, and the tax authority's job is to find the inconsistency between them.

## The number that goes wrong most often

Gross weight. It appears on the packing list, on the customs declaration, on the carton label, and on the commercial invoice in some formats. Four places, one number, and it is measured at different moments by different people.

On one shipment of 504 units, the carton label read 39.9 lb per carton and the packing list carried 39.7 lb. Over 28 cartons that is a difference of about 5.6 lb, which is nothing physically and everything administratively: two documents disagreeing about the same carton.

The resolution is not to average them. It is to decide which measurement governs, change the other documents to match, and record the reason. In that case the packing list figure was correct and the carton label was the outlier, so the documents were aligned on 504.0 kg total gross and the label was corrected.

The lesson generalises: a weight on a carton label is a factory measurement, and the figure on the declaration is the one customs will weigh against. They are allowed to differ by a rounding error and not by 0.2 lb per carton.

## The second number: cartons

Container counts, carton counts and quantity per carton are the same fact expressed three ways, and they are easy to get wrong when an order ships in two batches or gains a spare carton.

A packing list saying 23 cartons while the contract says 28 is the kind of discrepancy that stops a container, because the carrier's document now describes a different physical consignment from the one the buyer ordered.

The fix is arithmetic on the packing list: units ordered, units per carton, cartons required, and a line for spare units. If the spare units add a carton, the carton count changes and the totals change with it.

## The third: model and tariff code

The invoice, the declaration and the carton label all identify the product. If the invoice says one model code and the carton says another, the declaration cannot connect them.

The same applies to the tariff code. [The code is not settled for this product category](/blog/hs-code-ipl-devices), which makes consistency between documents more important rather than less: once the broker has chosen a code, every document carries that code and the description that goes with it.

## Which document gets asked for when

The sequence matters more than the list, because each stage only accepts a document that exists at that moment.

| Stage | What is asked for | Why it matters then |
|---|---|---|
| Before production | Invoice or proforma, with the Incoterm | The order terms are fixed here |
| Before booking | Packing list with weights and dimensions | The carrier charges on the measured volume |
| Before declaration | Invoice, packing list, origin data, declared elements | The declaration is built from these |
| At handover | The transport document | Nothing moves without it |
| At destination | Invoice, packing list, origin certificate, conformity documents | Release and duty |

The packing list is the document that gets underestimated. It is treated as an internal sheet, and it is the one the carrier and the destination authority both read.

## Where the conformity documents sit

The five travel documents get the goods there. They do not get them on sale.

Conformity documents — a declaration of conformity, a test report, a product certificate — are separate and are checked by a different audience. But they share the same identifiers: model code, manufacturer name, and often the batch. A shipment whose invoice says one model and whose certificate says another has two problems, not one.

## What to check before the container is sealed

1. Quantity: units, cartons, units per carton, and any spare units line.
2. Weights: net and gross, per carton and total, identical on packing list, declaration and carton label.
3. Dimensions: per carton, and the pallet or carton configuration actually loaded.
4. Identification: model code identical on invoice, packing list, label and declaration.
5. Tariff code and description: identical everywhere, and matching the goods.
6. Origin: consistent between certificate, declaration and the purchase order.

Six checks, ten minutes, before the doors close. The same six after the doors close cost a demurrage day.
