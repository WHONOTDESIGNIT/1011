**Short answer:** the grafted area and the donor area wait for the clinic to clear them, and stay out of use for the whole healing window. Keeping clear of the surgical area is a necessary action, and the requirement goes further than that: for the first weeks the boundary between grafted and untreated skin is hard to see, because crusting, redness and new growth all blur it, so the clinic draws that line and a patient who estimates it is guessing. Whether another body area can be treated depends on the healing stage, the medication in the patient's system and her individual response. That judgement belongs to her doctor. This article sets out what she should leave the clinic holding, and whom to ask.

## Why the manual will not answer it

A device manual states who should not use the device in general terms. It does not know that this patient had grafts placed three weeks ago, what medication she is on, or how her donor area is healing.

When the manual is silent on a specific case, patients fill the gap with a search result. That is how a two-month-old transplant ends up under a home device.

## The two zones

| Area | Who decides | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Grafted area | Clinic | Graft survival is a clinical question, and the clinic draws the boundary. A patient estimating that boundary is guessing. |
| Donor area, back of the head | Clinic | It is a wound in its own right. Patients leave it out because nothing was planted there. |
| Rest of the body | Clinic, judged per person | Antibiotics, painkillers, anticoagulants, photosensitising drugs, a keloid history and an active infection act on the person. The leg can be an unsafe site on a body that would take treatment elsewhere. |

This table divides responsibility. All three rows name the same judge, and each row gives a different reason: two of them are wounds, and the third is the patient's own state.

## What to hand over at discharge

One page, in the patient's language, using the clinic's wording:

- The treated area is excluded until the follow-up date.
- The follow-up date, written as a calendar date. A phrase like "when healed" gives the patient nothing to check.
- Which medications slow healing or make skin photosensitive, and so delay any light-based treatment.
- Who to ask, by role, if a device seller says otherwise.

That last line is the one patients use. A seller will not overrule a named person on a page.

## Why clinics should care commercially

A patient who damages a graft with a home device blames the transplant. The clinical cost lands on the clinic that did the work, long after the aftercare conversation was skipped because it felt like a sales moment.

One printed page is cheaper than that conversation happening later, badly.

## What you will have to justify to the clinic owner

A one-page discharge instruction looks like admin. The case for it is three numbers the clinic already has.

Post-procedure enquiries: how many patients call within ninety days asking about hair removal or light devices. Each call is time a coordinator spends, and each one is a question the clinic could have answered at discharge.

Revision consultations: how many of those calls turn into a graft concern that turns out to be unrelated to the procedure. The clinic pays for that consultation either way.

Complaint cost: a conversation about a damaged graft area starts with the clinic. The device maker hears about it later, if at all. Put the clinic's own hourly cost against the print run of a page.

## How to say it without sounding defensive

"Read this as a list of things to avoid with a device we did not sell you. It says nothing about the quality of the work."

That framing keeps the page in the aftercare pack. A legal disclaimer gets ignored. The clinics that use it report the same thing: patients read a page that names a date, and skip a page that says "consult your doctor".

## If the clinic decides to stock devices

Then the discharge page has to name the device as well as the category. A patient told "no light devices" while holding a box she just bought has been told nothing.

Write the page with the product in front of you: the treatment areas, the schedule, and the follow-up date where the conversation reopens. One page, one date, one person to ask.

That is the difference between a clinic that sells devices and one that sells devices and then manages the consequences. ## The one thing to check on the device

If a patient brings her own device to the consultation, ask her to show you the manual page that lists contraindications. Most manuals list tattoos, medication and broken skin. Few name a recent transplant, which is exactly why the clinic's own page has to.

That check takes a minute and it tells you whether the patient has read anything at all. It also tells you which advice she has already been given, which is the part you cannot see from your side of the table.
