**Short answer:** the warning text sets the layout. It differs per market, and it is reviewed by someone outside your studio. Ask for the market list and the approved text before you plate.

## Four things a printer gets wrong on this job

One text for every market. The wording for an electrical device changes by country.

The front panel gets laid out first, because the front is the easy panel. The back or side panel carries the warnings, the importer block, the barcode and the manufacturer name, and that is where the fit fails.

Text treated as an image: reviewed text changes, and once it is baked into a flattened file, every revision is a new file from the brand.

And quoting before the market list exists. A label covering a small market set and a label covering a large one are different jobs with the same SKU code.

## What to ask for, in order

| Ask | Because |
|---|---|
| The full market list for this SKU | Sets which text blocks must fit |
| The approved text per market, as live text | It will be revised at least once |
| Panel dimensions with bleed and the barcode position | Determines what fits on the back panel |
| The language set, and who checks it | A wrong translation in a warning block is a compliance problem |
| Material and finish | Matters for a device that lives in a bathroom |

## The panel that decides the run

Measure the back panel, then measure the longest text among the markets. If the two do not fit at the required point size, the job needs one of three things: a folded leaflet, a wider pack, or a reduced claim set. Each one needs the brand to say yes.

Shrinking the type to make it fit creates the compliance problem.

## Barcodes and quiet zones

A retail barcode needs a clear quiet zone, and a device pack puts it next to a fold. Registration drifts. Plan the barcode on a flat area, away from the fold, with the quiet zone measured in millimetres.

## What you will have to justify to the brand

A printer flagging a layout problem gets asked to solve it. Come with options and a price for each.

A folded leaflet carries more paper and more assembly, and the warnings come off the panel that gets squeezed.

A wider pack brings a new dieline and a new plate set, and it settles the problem for every market after this one.

The cheapest of the three is a reduced claim set for the smallest market, and it needs the brand's compliance route to agree in writing.

## The number that decides it

Plate cost against reprint cost. A plate set is paid once per revision, and a reprint is paid per run.

Which one is cheaper depends on whether the run repeats. At plating time nobody knows, so both numbers go on one page and the brand chooses.

## Where the detail comes from

- [ISO 3864](https://www.iso.org/standard/51015.html) — the safety-sign and warning-symbol standard a label block follows.
- [Regulation (EU) 2023/988](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/988/oj/eng) — the general product safety rules that cover the consumer device.
- [European Commission](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/sectors/cosmetics_en) — the Commission overview of the cosmetics rules a gel falls under.

## FAQ

**What sets the panel layout on an IPL label?**

The warning text for the longest market. You measure the back panel and the longest text among the markets, and if the two do not fit at the required point size, the options are a folded leaflet, a wider pack, or a reduced claim set.

**What should a printer request before quoting a label job?**

The full market list for the SKU, the approved text per market as live text, panel dimensions with bleed and the barcode position, the language set and who checks it, and the material and finish.

**Why does approved warning text stay as live text?**

Reviewed text changes, and text baked into a flattened file makes every revision a new file from the brand.

**Who signs the warning text, and when?**

The brand's reviewer. A warning block goes to translation after that reviewer signs it.
