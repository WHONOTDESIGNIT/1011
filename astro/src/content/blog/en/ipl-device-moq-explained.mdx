An IPL device MOQ is arithmetic, not a house rule. ODM projects usually start at 500 units and OEM at 1,000 to 5,000. Those numbers aren't arbitrary. They're the point where fixed costs spread thin enough that the per-unit price stops looking embarrassing.

I'm Eric, co-founder of iShine. We run ODM and OEM programs for brands like Costco and Haier, which means the MOQ arithmetic on this page is the one we argue about internally.

Once you see the formula, the whole conversation changes. You stop arguing about the MOQ and start arguing about the fixed costs, which is where the money actually is.

## The formula nobody writes down

MOQ = fixed cost ÷ the per-unit amortization the factory is willing to carry.

That's it. The factory has a target: the first order should recover the fixed spend, or at least most of it, because the second order might never come. If tooling and certification come to $45,000 and the factory wants no more than $90 of fixed cost sitting in each unit, the MOQ is 500.

Change either side and the number moves. This is why two factories quote different MOQs for what looks like the same product. They're not being arbitrary. They're carrying different fixed costs, or different appetite for risk.

## Four fixed costs behind every MOQ

**Tooling.** The housing mold set is the big one, $20,000 to $50,000 depending on part count, finish and whether you need a second color. It's a one-time spend that produces nothing until it's cut.

**Certification.** Safety, EMC and photobiological testing are billed per standard and per sample. The lab doesn't care whether you're making 500 units or 5,000. This is the cost that hurts most at low volume, because it doesn't compress. The two standards behind that line are [IEC 60335-2-113](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/67733) for the appliance itself and [IEC 62471](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/7076) for photobiological safety. Neither gets cheaper at 500 units, and a US launch adds an [FDA 510(k)](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfpmn/pmn.cfm) on top. A European launch adds [Regulation (EU) 2017/745](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj) instead, and it is priced the same way: per standard, per sample.

**Packaging plates.** Print plates and dielines for a custom gift box. Modest on their own, but they get re-cut every time the artwork changes, which at low volume is often.

**Setup and first-article approval.** Line changeover, jig setup, and the first-article inspection that has to happen before the run is released.

Only one thing in that list scales with unit count. Everything else is a rock.

## What actually changes across 500, 1,000 and 5,000

Here's a worked example using round numbers, not a real quote. Fixed costs of $45,000 ($30,000 tooling and $15,000 certification) against a variable cost of $11 per unit for parts and assembly.

| Units | Fixed per unit | Variable per unit | Total per unit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 500 | $90 | $11 | $101 |
| 1,000 | $45 | $11 | $56 |
| 3,000 | $15 | $11 | $26 |
| 5,000 | $9 | $11 | $20 |

The variable cost barely moves. Every dollar of difference between the columns is fixed cost looking for somewhere to land.

This is why a brand ordering 500 units and a brand ordering 5,000 units of the same product can be quoted prices that differ by a factor of five, and neither factory is lying. They're answering different questions.

## Two levers that genuinely lower MOQ

**Share an existing mold.** If your housing can live inside a platform the factory already runs, the $30,000 disappears from your side of the table and the MOQ can drop to a few hundred. The trade-off is real: your product shares a silhouette with whatever else that mold produces. For some brands that's fine. For a brand whose entire pitch is industrial design, it isn't.

**Use standard packaging for the first run.** A plain printed carton instead of a molded gift box removes the plate cost and the minimum print run. You can upgrade packaging on order two, once you know the product sells.

Everything else people suggest, like "can't you just do 200?", is asking the factory to eat the fixed cost and hope. Occasionally a factory will, for a relationship it wants. It will price that hope into your unit cost.

## When a low MOQ is a warning

A MOQ below the arithmetic floor has to be paid for somehow. Four ways it usually is:

**Second-hand tooling.** The mold exists because someone else paid for it and walked away. The housing may be fine. The tooling may be at the end of its life, which shows up as flash and sink marks on unit 800.

**Refurbished or salvaged cells.** IPL heads have a limited shot count. A cheap unit may be built around components that were already used.

**Borrowed certification.** A factory that can't amortize its own certificate may be shipping under someone else's. That works until a customs officer asks whose name is on the report.

**A price that expires.** The first order is cheap because it's a loss leader, and the second order arrives at the real price after you've committed to the packaging and the brand assets.

None of these are visible in the quote. All of them are visible in the [seven documents we tell brands to request](/blog/how-to-vet-ipl-device-manufacturer) before paying a deposit.

## Phasing an order so MOQ stops blocking you

The MOQ is a fixed-cost problem, so solve it like one. Three approaches that work:

Take the shared mold and spend the saved money on a better housing finish later, when volume justifies a new tool. Buy the standard packaging for run one and design the retail box for run two. And if you genuinely can't hit the floor, ask whether the factory will hold the tooling and release it in two tranches — the fixed cost gets recovered across both, and you don't carry 5,000 units of inventory in your first quarter.

What you should not do is accept a suspiciously low MOQ without asking which of the four fixed costs got moved somewhere else. It didn't vanish. It's in the unit price, or it's in your future.
