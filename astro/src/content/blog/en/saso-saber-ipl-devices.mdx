**Short answer:** Saudi Arabia does not issue one conformity certificate. SABER produces a product certificate valid for about a year and a shipment certificate for each consignment. Since 2025 the shipment certificate has to be in hand before the goods reach the port, and that deadline has cost importers more than any test failure.

## SABER is a platform, not a certification body

The name gets used as though SABER certifies products. It doesn't. It is the online system through which two different documents are issued, and the difference between them is where the planning goes wrong.

| | Product Certificate of Conformity | Shipment Certificate of Conformity |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | The product, for about a year | One consignment |
| Reused | Yes, across shipments | No, every shipment |
| Depends on | Test evidence and a technical file | A valid product certificate plus shipping documents |
| Issued through | SABER, by the importer or a recognised body | SABER, per consignment |
| Expires | On its own date | On arrival, effectively |

A manufacturer who plans for "the SASO certificate" usually plans for the first column and forgets the second. The second is the one with the deadline.

## The arrival rule

Since 2025, the shipment certificate has to be issued before the goods arrive at a Saudi port. A certificate applied for after arrival is treated as invalid, and the goods do not clear.

This changed the shape of the planning. It used to be possible to start the shipment certificate while the vessel was at sea and finish before the container moved. Now the sequence is inverted: certificate first, then arrival. For a factory shipping on a schedule, that means the paperwork has to be complete before the booking, not after it.

Two practical consequences:

- A late product certificate does not delay the product certificate. It delays **every future shipment**, because nothing can be certified without a valid reference.
- Air freight makes the window tighter, not looser. A five-day transit leaves no room to discover a missing document.

## What a CB report buys, and what it does not

A valid CB certificate and report can be accepted as the test basis for some products, which is the difference between a few weeks and a few months. It is not a substitute for the Saudi file, though.

Two national differences come up every time with a mains-powered beauty appliance:

- **The plug.** Saudi plugs follow their own standard, and a European plug in the box is not compliant regardless of what the CB report says. This is a product change, not a paperwork change.
- **The supply.** 220 V at 60 Hz. An adapter rated only for 50 Hz is a specification problem, and the rating has to be visible on the marking.

So the CB report shortens the testing and leaves the hardware questions untouched. That is worth stating plainly, because buyers sometimes treat the CB certificate as the whole gate.

## Arabic labelling, and what counts

Labels and user manuals must include Arabic. Bilingual is accepted; Arabic-only is not required. What matters is that the Arabic is on the product and in the manual, not in a separate leaflet added at the importer's warehouse.

For an IPL device the manual is where the risk sits, because it carries the warnings and the contraindications. The wording in a regulated market is not a translation exercise, and a literal rendering of an English manual will often be rejected by the importer's own reviewer before SABER is ever involved.

## The timeline, stage by stage

These are the platform's published service standards plus what happens around them, so treat the numbers as planning figures rather than guarantees.

| Stage | Typical duration | What decides it |
|---|---|---|
| CB report transfer, if usable | 2 to 4 weeks | Whether the report matches the shipped configuration |
| Fresh testing, if not | 4 to 8 weeks | Scope of standards required |
| Product certificate registration | Around 8 days published | Completeness of the technical file |
| Shipment certificate | Around 3 days published | PCoC reference, invoice, packing list, bill of lading |
| Total to first shipment | 3 to 6 weeks | Mostly the testing stage |

The bottom row is the one to put in front of a customer who asks when the first container can leave.

## Who holds what

SABER is operated in practice by the Saudi importer, or by a recognised conformity body acting for the exporter. That makes this the fifth market in this series with the same structural point: the entity inside the territory is the gate.

The EU has its responsible person, the United Kingdom has its importer, Japan has its importer filing with METI, Australia has its sponsor, the Eurasian union requires a resident applicant, and Saudi Arabia requires an entity that can operate SABER. Different names, same shape.

For a brand with no Saudi presence, the importer is therefore not a logistics detail. They are the party who can issue the documents, and the relationship decides how fast a shipment can leave.
