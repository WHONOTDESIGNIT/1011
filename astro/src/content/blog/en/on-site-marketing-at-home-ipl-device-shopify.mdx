# How to Market an At-Home IPL Hair Removal Device on Shopify

Two things break most IPL stores before the ad account is even warm. Shopify Payments refuses medical devices, and the ad platforms treat a hair removal device as a health product. Neither problem has anything to do with your logo, your theme, or your discount code.

I've watched this play out from the supplier side for years. Brands order a container of handsets, launch a Shopify store with a beautiful hero image, run Meta ads for two weeks, get rejected or get no sales, and conclude the niche is dead. The niche isn't dead. Most people sell one box where customers are quietly buying a twelve-week protocol. That gap is where the money sits.

> **The short answer.** The stores that work sell a twelve-week treatment protocol instead of a device, prove fit before asking for $200, keep the offer compliant for Shopify Payments and Google Merchant Center, and use email to carry a buyer through weeks one to twelve. Everything below is the detail behind those four moves.

## The problem at the center of this product

A customer buys once. Then they use the thing for years. Our own handsets ship with 900,000 flashes, and the specs sheet puts the lamp at thirty years of use at the recommended cadence of one session a week. Either number is absurd in beauty tech. Nothing else on the shelf in that aisle lasts a decade.

Sit with that. You have no replenishment cycle, no consumable refill, and one of the highest price points in beauty tech. Acquisition cost gets paid exactly once and never recovered on a reorder. Any marketing plan that assumes repeat purchase is fantasy. Four things move the number: conversion rate on a cold visitor, average order value, retention of the first purchase long enough to earn a review and a referral, and paid traffic that doesn't get banned.

## Set up the store before you write a single ad

Payment eligibility comes first, because it changes your entire checkout.

[Shopify Payments eligibility](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/payments/shopify-payments/onboarding/eligibility) excludes "products or services that require regulatory approval or compliance, such as cannabis and related products, prescription drugs, medical devices." A US-marketed powered hair removal handset is a [Class II device under 21 CFR 878.4810](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfcfr/cfrsearch.cfm?fr=878.4810), product code OHT. Read those two sentences together and you get the punchline: the gateway a Shopify store leans on by default is closed to a chunk of this category.

Practically, that means applying for a third-party gateway early, before launch, so you aren't writing product descriptions while a payment application sits in review. Klarna, Affirm and PayPal matter more here than in most categories anyway. At a $200 to $400 price point, splitting the payment into four is often the difference between a sale and an abandoned cart, and the installment badge on the product page pulls more weight than the processor logo next to it.

Shopify's own [Acceptable Use Policy](https://www.shopify.com/legal/aup) is blunt about where the risk lands. The platform gives notice and product-level enforcement, but the AUP says plainly that the merchant is "responsible for following the rules of your platform, your partners, and the law," and that "the most common product eligibility disputes originate from outside Shopify," from regulators and rights holders. In this category, the takedown rarely starts with Shopify. It starts with a competitor, a customer complaint, or an ad review.

Then the boring mechanical part. Model your variants the way phone case stores model handsets. Build the fit layer into the product template: a skin tone and hair color selector, a contraindications block, and a visible answer set on what the device will not do. That last one sounds like self-sabotage and does the opposite. IPL doesn't work well on white, grey or red hair, and the skin tone range has a hard ceiling. A customer who discovers that after delivery becomes a return and a chargeback. A customer who discovers it in a fit checker becomes a filter that protects your margin.

Warranty, lamp lifespan and the returns window belong above the fold on the product page, not buried in an FAQ accordion. In a category that gets credit card disputes from buyers who didn't read, visibility of the returns promise is a conversion element.

## On-site keyword strategy for IPL stores

Cost per click in this niche is not friendly, which makes owned content the cheapest traffic you will ever get. Here's the part most stores skip: the search bar knows almost nothing about your brand, so every page has to earn its traffic on a question the customer is already asking.

The keyword map that matters in this category splits into four layers.

**Problem keywords.** "How to remove facial hair without shaving every day," "why does my hair grow back so fast after waxing." Search volume is softer and intent is sharper, because the person typing it is unhappy and looking for a way out.

**Mechanism and protocol keywords.** "How does IPL work," "how often should I use an IPL device," "IPL results after 4 weeks," "IPL before and after 12 weeks." This is where the buying decision gets made. Our own customer questions land on exactly these: how long until results, does it hurt, which intensity to start at, what do I do after a session. A store that answers those questions in full owns the customer's mental checklist before they land on the product page.

**Comparison and status keywords.** "IPL vs laser at home," "IPL vs epilator," "best IPL device for dark skin," "is IPL worth it." Higher competition, higher intent, and where the money terms live.

**Ecommerce-layer keywords.** These are the ones B2B sellers forget: "IPL device bulk," "private label IPL," "IPL dropshipping," "IPL device supplier." They pull brand owners and sourcing agents, not consumers, and one wholesale enquiry is worth a hundred retail sessions.

Two structural notes on how to build it. Give the skin-tone suitability question its own page rather than a paragraph, because it's both a high-volume query and a returns reducer. Keep every article one click from the product page it supports. A user landing on a protocol article and then hitting a dead end has to restart the journey in the search bar, and half of them won't.

## Paid acquisition with the policy handbrake on

Google and Meta will take money in this category. They just make you qualify first, and the qualification has to be completed before the campaign, not after the disapproval email.

For Google Merchant Center, the classification on the feed is the whole game. A device presented as a medical tool needs the registration and labelling to match what the feed declares, and the offer page needs the manufacturer, the model, the intended use and the safety information to be findable. Vague feed data in a regulated category is the fastest route to a suspended account, and account suspensions cost far more than the initial application.

Meta's health and personal care advertising rules restrict the before-and-after imagery the beauty category runs on. Plan creative that doesn't depend on it: the routine, the prep, the arm holding the device, the half of the leg that was shaved this morning. This constraint is why creator content converts here. A person demonstrating the twelve-week cadence is compliant, and it reads as a protocol rather than a claim.

TikTok is where the price-sensitive end of this market lives, and the format fits. Before-and-after shock content gets removed, but "how I fit a session into a Sunday evening" doesn't. Ulike built a large part of its consumer business on short-form video, and its US storefront runs on Shopify, which is one sign the platform can carry this category once payments and feed data are handled properly.

One budget rule for the first ninety days: spend on retargeting before you spend on cold traffic. The research window for this purchase is long, sometimes measured in weeks, and a visitor who read three pages is a far better use of a pound than a stranger who saw a video once.

## Email, the only channel that can hold a twelve-week customer

Most stores send a purchase confirmation, a shipping notification, and a review request at day seven. For this product, day seven is when the customer has used the device once and decided nothing.

Build the flow around the protocol instead:

Week 1, setup and shaving technique, plus which intensity to start on. Week 3, the check-in where you set expectations honestly, because the first visible change usually shows up around the third or fourth session. Week 8 to 12, the review request, timed for when the change is visible rather than when the parcel arrives. Then a refill-style offer, which in this category means accessories. Replacement flashes where the model supports them, a razor, a cooling gel, a case, travel pouch, spare adapters.

That's also where the average order value gets repaired. The device is a one-time sale, so the accessories and the routine products carry the second transaction. Our own production work on this is on the [IPL handset range](https://iplmanufacturer.com/products) and the [private label programmes](https://iplmanufacturer.com/services) that back it, and the pattern is consistent: brands that ship an accessory in the box at cost sell more of them later, because the customer has already learned to use it.

## What the biggest-brand comparison misses

Braun and Philips sell in the same stores as the challengers and lose the spec war on paper. Higher energy output, denser filter stacks, three-year warranties. What they can't do is move as fast on culture.

That's the opening. RoseSkinCo ran the playbook most successfully in this category by selling a routine rather than a device, bundling the shaver and the aftercare lotion with the handset and putting the whole thing on a page that reads like a treatment plan. Ulike went the other way, leaning on device design and video volume. Both work. Neither is a feature list.

Here's the uncomfortable implication for anyone building a store right now. There is no way to differentiate on flash count, joules, or filter wavelength, because the customer cannot feel any of it before purchase. You differentiate on fit, on the protocol, on the speed of the content you publish, and on how visible your safety information is.

## A pre-launch compliance pass that saves the relaunch

Run this before you spend on traffic, not after the first account suspension.

Confirm the specific model you sell holds its own regulatory clearance or registration in the markets you ship to. Clearance belongs to a manufacturer and a model. It doesn't transfer because a supplier says "FDA cleared" in a WhatsApp message.

Substantiate every results claim with test data on file. The [FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) requires that health-related claims be backed by competent and reliable scientific evidence, and "results in two weeks" is a health claim. The same guidance kills testimonials presented as typical outcomes. Save the outlier customer story, use the median one.

Save "permanent" for reduction. A brand can say permanent hair reduction, and that is the phrase your copy should use. Claiming that hair goes away for good is a clearance claim, and it belongs only to a model that actually holds that indication.

Put contraindications where people read them: pregnancy, breastfeeding, tattoos, moles, photosensitising medication, active skin conditions. Add the boundary line that tells readers to consult a dermatologist before use if they have a skin condition. Cheap to write, expensive to omit.

Label the device honestly. Skin tone range, hair color limits, intended use, manufacturer name and address. Then check the same information appears on the offer page that receives the ad traffic.

Finally, the [MAUDE database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfmaude/search.cfm) is public and worth an hour of your time before launch. Reading adverse event reports for light-based over-the-counter hair removal devices tells you which complaints recur, which are device faults and which are user error from unclear instructions. Most of the second category is fixable on your own product page and in your own manual.

## Questions we get from brand owners

**Can I use Shopify Payments if I sell an IPL device?**
Often no. Shopify Payments treats medical devices as a prohibited category, and a US-marketed powered hair removal handset is classified as a Class II device. Apply for a third-party gateway before launch, and check the prohibited-business list for the specific country you're selling in, because it varies by region.

**How long does it take to see results, and how should I phrase it?**
Our handsets show noticeable reduction after three to four sessions and full results after around twelve. Say it that way, keep the test data on file, and add the honest caveat that results depend on hair coarseness and the area treated. Vaguer promises convert slightly better once and lose the customer at the chargeback.

**What content should I write first?**
The suitability page. Skin tone and hair color fit is the single question that decides whether the sale happens and whether the product comes back. Build the checker, write the long explanation behind it, then write the protocol pages that feed it.

**Do I need a subscription app?**
Only if the offer actually needs one, and in this category it usually doesn't. The recurring value sits in accessories and in content that keeps the customer treating consistently for twelve weeks. Pushing a subscription onto a device with a thirty-year lamp is a churn machine with extra steps.

**How do I handle returns without killing margin?**
Show the fit checker before checkout, ship a clear pre-use instruction sheet, and make the returns window and warranty visible. A percentage of returns is cheaper than the review damage from a customer who used the wrong intensity for six weeks because nothing on the page told them not to.
