**Short answer:** freight is charged on the volume somebody measures, not the volume a carton drawing predicts. On a 504-unit shipment, 28 master cartons at 18 units each, the calculated figure was 2.13 cubic metres and the forwarder's warehouse measured 2.29. That gap is about 7.5 percent, and it is the difference between a quote that holds and one that comes back higher.

## The two numbers, side by side

| | Calculated at the factory | Measured at the forwarder |
|---|---|---|
| Total volume | 2.13 CBM | 2.29 CBM |
| Per master carton | 0.0761 CBM | 0.0818 CBM |
| Per unit (504 units) | 0.00423 CBM | 0.00454 CBM |
| Difference | — | **+7.5%** |

Both numbers are honest. They are measurements of different things.

The first is arithmetic: carton length × width × height × 28. It is what the carton is supposed to be. The second is what happens to 28 cartons once they are stacked, filmed and put on a pallet in a warehouse.

## Where the 7.5 percent comes from

A corrugated carton is not a rigid box. Stacked three or four high, the walls flex and the corners take the load. A carton that measures 48.5 × 43 × 36.5 on a table measures slightly more once it has been compressed under other cartons and released, because the board does not return to a perfect rectangle.

Four things add to the measured figure:

- **Bulging.** Cartons compress under load and bow outward at the faces. Multiply a small bulge by 28 cartons and it shows up in the total.
- **Film and pallet.** Stretch wrap adds a skin, and a pallet adds height that is not in the carton arithmetic.
- **Alignment.** Cartons never stack perfectly square. A few millimetres of drift per carton is centimetres at the top of the stack.
- **The measuring method.** A forwarder measures a pallet footprint and height, not a theoretical stack. That is the number the airline or the shipping line will use.

None of that is waste or error. It is the difference between a drawing and a warehouse.

## The 7.5 percent rule in practice

Customers often buy by volume band: a rate that changes at one cubic metre, three cubic metres, five. When the band boundary is what the customer is optimising, a 7.5 percent surprise on the wrong side costs more than the volume itself.

So we plan backwards. If a customer needs to fill exactly one cubic metre, we build the order to about **0.93 CBM of nominal carton volume**, which lands at roughly 1.00 CBM once measured. The order fills the band without spilling over it.

The same arithmetic works in reverse for a quote that has to be conservative: take the carton-spec volume, multiply by 1.075, round up to the next carton, and quote on that. A supplier who quotes on nominal volume is either under-quoting by 7.5 percent or will come back for the difference.

## What it does to freight per unit

Freight per unit is not carton volume divided by units. It is chargeable volume times rate, divided by units, where chargeable volume is the measured figure.

| Quantity | Chargeable CBM at 7.5% | Freight per unit at USD 120/CBM |
|---|---|---|
| 504 units (28 cartons) | 2.29 | ~0.545 |
| 1,008 units (56 cartons) | 4.58 | ~0.545 |
| 5,040 units (280 cartons) | 22.90 | ~0.545 |

The per-unit figure stays flat because both the volume and the units scale together. That is the point of putting the arithmetic in a table: the number that looks like it should move, doesn't, and the number people assume is fixed, the chargeable volume, is the one that drifts.

What does move the per-unit figure is how well the carton is filled. A master carton designed around 18 units that ships with 15 is paying for 20 percent air, and no amount of negotiating the rate recovers that.

## Where buyers get this wrong

Three patterns, all common.

**Quoting on the carton spec.** The drawing says 0.056 cubic metres, so the calculation uses it, and the invoice arrives 7.5 percent higher. The buyer reads it as a rate increase rather than as a measurement difference.

**Ignoring the pallet.** Air freight measures the pallet, not the cartons. A pallet that is 10 cm taller than the arithmetic predicts is volume the shipper pays for.

**Optimising the wrong number.** Negotiating the rate per cubic metre while the carton contains 20 percent air is fixing the smaller of the two numbers.

## What to ask a supplier

Four questions, and the answers tell you whether the CBM on the quotation was calculated or measured:

1. Does this volume come from the carton specification or from a measured pallet?
2. What expansion did you apply, and is it in the figure?
3. How many units per master carton, and is the carton full?
4. At what quantity does the shipping band change, and does this order land under it?

A supplier who answers the first two from memory has done this before. A supplier who says "the carton is 0.056 CBM" has given you a drawing.
