Philips has sold the Lumea 9000 series for years. Braun has sold the Silk·expert Pro 5 for years. Neither brand ships a new platform every season, and both keep winning the shelf.

Call it laziness if you like. It is also a decision about what a home device is for.

## Three products that got too good to sell

The three cases below are not a conspiracy theory. The Phoebus arrangement is documented history; the pen and the fan are the same pattern showing up without anyone coordinating it. That is what makes the pattern worth taking seriously.

**Light bulbs.** In the 1920s, bulbs could run past 2,500 hours. Manufacturers found that longer life meant fewer replacements, so the Phoebus cartel, formed in 1924, coordinated a 1,000-hour ceiling across major producers. Durability was capped to protect the replacement cycle.

**Ballpoint pens.** Early pens leaked and died young, so people bought often. Once tips and ink improved, one pen lasted until the ink ran out. The industry answered with refills, barrel designs and collaborations, which is a polite way of saying it invented reasons to buy a pen that still worked.

**Fans and white goods.** Motors and plastics improved until a fan ran ten years. Replacement demand collapsed, and growth moved to renovation and style upgrades. The smart fan and the bladeless fan exist because the old fan would not break.

When a product stops wearing out, the replacement cycle moves somewhere else: design, status, accessories, or a new category.

## The durability trap

An IPL device is closer to a fan than to a serum. It has a lamp with a rated flash count, a cooling system, a power supply and firmware. It is built to last, and a well-made unit lasts years.

That creates a trap for challengers. The obvious way to look new is to push the specification: more flashes, faster firing, higher energy. The obvious way to pay for that is to raise the price. Do both and the product drifts toward the salon machine it was supposed to replace.

The home IPL buyer is not shopping for a clinic. She is escaping one. Part of the appeal is never sharing a machine that has been against someone else's skin. A device that costs like a salon treatment and looks like one gives that up.

## The flagships, and why they hold

| Brand | Flagship family | Model codes | What carries over |
|---|---|---|---|
| Philips | Lumea 9000 series | BRI97x (BRI973, BRI977 and market variants) | Same lamp platform, SenseIQ skin sensor, cordless body, attachment ecosystem |
| Philips | Lumea 8000 series | BRI937/02 and siblings | Volume platform below the flagship, shared consumables |
| Braun | Silk·expert Pro 5 | PL5xxx | Same body since launch, attachments and caps across years |
| Braun | Skin i·expert | PL7xxx | Newer premium tier, same refill logic |

The details differ by market and year. The strategy does not. Both brands run one platform for a long time, sell attachments and cartridges into it, and let certification and service carry across generations. A buyer who owns the cap or the case has a reason to stay inside the family.

For a manufacturer, this is the awkward part. Long platforms are cheap to certify and expensive to copy. A competitor cannot take the shelf by adding a feature, because the feature is not what the buyer is loyal to.

There is a second reason the shelf rewards the long platform, and it is boring. Retail listings, warranty reserves, spare parts and service scripts all get cheaper per unit the longer a model stays in market. A brand that replaces its platform every season pays the certification bill every season, and it pays it before a single unit sells. Philips and Braun have turned that cost into a moat: their service network knows the device, their accessories fit it, and their packaging does not need a new die line every year.

## The ULIKE trap

ULIKE did the opposite. Faster flashes, higher flash counts, a device that behaves like a CPU with a new generation every year.

Consumers stopped following. The product did not get worse; it got more expensive and more clinical at the same time. The pitch moved from "your own device at home" to "a better machine," and the second pitch belongs to a different buyer with a different budget.

That is the part the specification race misses. A phone upgrade is sold on the camera; a home IPL is sold on not going to a clinic. Speed up the flash and you improve the device while moving it away from the reason it was bought.

The brand improves the specification, raises the price, and repositions itself out of the customer's life.

## The casting tells you the strategy

Look at the campaigns. Philips Lumea and Braun have run imagery with darker skin, white skin, short hair, rounder bodies. None of that casting is accidental. A mass-market brand is doing the arithmetic of who buys home devices: migrants, working women, and women over 35 who are done with appointments.

Sensica went the other way. Muscle tone, classical skin, balanced proportions, a composed smile. Traditional beauty, shot with intent. Where Philips photographs a market, Sensica photographs a woman who already knows what she wants and does not need a caption explaining it. That is a harder image to build and a much harder one to copy.

You can argue about which is more honest. Honesty is debatable; differentiation is not. Sensica has a visual position a copycat cannot steal by lowering a price, and that is why we think it has a real path into the top three. If it reaches number one, the reason will be discipline, not the lamp.

## The cockroach niche

Most small brands and contract factories in this category are not building a brand. They are copying the last Philips launch, undercutting it, and hoping the shelf does not notice.

Think of it as an ecological niche rather than a strategy. In any mature category there is a low-margin layer that survives by being cheap, fast and replaceable: it absorbs price competition, it takes the volume the flagship will not chase, and it dies when the environment changes. Biologists call that a niche, not a business. The copier is adapted to a specific condition, a flagship launch it can copy, and it has no answer when the condition disappears.

It is a hard way to make a living. No pricing power, no certification moat, no reason for a customer to return. The moment the copy is good enough, the copier competes with ten other copiers on the same specification.

Copying a flagship is a rent, not a strategy. The copy competes with ten other copies on the same specification, and the shelf has no reason to keep any of them.

## Four questions before buying the platform

Four questions separate a device that will still be supported in year three from one that will be orphaned.

How long has this platform been in market? A model with a two-year history has a parts supply, a service script and a firmware line. A model launched last quarter has none of those yet.

Does the certification cover the model on the box? A certificate with a different model number is a certificate for a different device, and a buyer who checks will find that out.

Are the accessories shared? Caps, cases, cartridges and gel that fit across generations are the cheapest retention mechanism a brand has, and they signal that the platform is meant to last.

Who owns the tooling? If the answer is not the brand that paid for it, the platform belongs to someone else.

## If you want a seat at the table

Three things decide whether a challenger survives.

**Pick a platform and keep it.** A device family that stays in market for four or five years amortises certification, tooling and service. Rebuilding the platform every season burns all three.

**Protect the price.** A home device that discounts to enter the market has no room to fund the certification that keeps it there. Price rigidity is the asset that pays for compliance.

**Choose an aesthetic and hold it.** The brands that last have a consistent picture of who buys them. Philips chose the mainstream. Sensica chose classical beauty. Both work. Neither works if it changes every campaign.
