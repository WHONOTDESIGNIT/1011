**Short answer:** printing a private label IPL box goes wrong in seven places, and each one is a missing or wrong file rather than a bad printer. Dieline, logo, colour, manual text, claims, barcode, and a signed proof. Get the seven right and the print run is boring, which is what it should be.

## The seven files

| # | File | What it must contain | What it costs to discover late |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dieline | Trim, bleed, fold and glue lines, at the printer's template | Plate remake |
| 2 | Logo | Vector, outlined or with fonts supplied, single colour and full colour versions | Redraw mid-plate |
| 3 | Colour | Pantone reference or CMYK values, plus the finish (matte, gloss, soft touch) | Whole run in the wrong shade |
| 4 | Manual text | Every required language, as text not images | Manual reprint, separate from the box |
| 5 | Claims and warnings | Market-specific block, legally reviewed | Overprinting or a sticker |
| 6 | Barcode | Correct symbology, correct number, placed inside the quiet zone | Rejected at the retailer's DC |
| 7 | Reference proof | A physical or signed digital proof both sides approve | Argument about what was approved |

## The three that actually cost money

**The logo arrives as a picture.** A PNG pulled from a website is 72 to 150 dots per inch. Print needs vector, or at minimum 300 dots per inch at final size. A logo that is even slightly soft is visible on a box in a way it never is on a screen, and the printer will either reject it or print it and let the buyer notice.

**The colour is specified as a screen colour.** RGB values do not survive the move to print. Ask for a Pantone reference, or CMYK values, and a named finish, because matte and gloss change how the same ink reads. Two suppliers can produce the same CMYK file and two visibly different boxes.

**The warnings are written for the wrong market.** A beauty device carries different regulatory text in the EU, the US, the UK and the Gulf, and the box is printed once. This is the file that most often arrives late, because it needs the compliance route settled before the artwork is frozen.

## The manual is not the box

It is worth separating the two, because the manual is where text volume lives and the box is where the visual work lives. A printed manual in several languages is a separate print job with its own platemaking, and it is the part that changes most often.

Two practical rules. Keep the manual as text files, not as a designed PDF with the text baked into images, so a single language can be revised without remaking the file. And freeze the manual's regulatory wording before the box artwork, because the box usually quotes the manual's warnings.

## The barcode and the marketplace label

A retail barcode and a marketplace label are different things and both may need to fit on the same panel.

- A retail barcode needs the right symbology, the correct number, and a clear quiet zone around it. Printers cannot always control registration closely enough for a barcode placed near a fold.
- A marketplace label, for a seller shipping into a warehouse network, carries its own identifier and is usually applied by the seller, not printed. Leave a flat, uncoated area for it, or the label falls off in a cold warehouse.

The quiet zone and the flat area are two millimetres of planning that save a reprint.

## What we ask a brand to send

| Item | Format we prefer | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Logo | AI, EPS or SVG; fonts outlined or supplied | One colour and full colour versions |
| Colour | Pantone numbers, or CMYK with a finish named | Include the target for the inside of the box |
| Manual text | Word or plain text per language | English source plus any translations the brand already has |
| Warnings | Text, per market | Reviewed by the brand's own compliance route |
| Barcode | Number plus symbology, or a supplied image at 300 dpi | We check placement, not the number's validity |
| Reference | Photos or an existing box we can hold | Faster than descriptions |
| Dieline sign-off | A single approver named in writing | Avoids two people approving different things |

That last row is not paperwork for its own sake. A project with two approvers and no named final one is a project that approves one design and prints another.

## What happens to the time

Artwork is usually the last thing a project waits for and the first thing it blames. A realistic sequence:

1. Dieline issued with the packaging quote.
2. Logo and colour confirmed against that dieline.
3. Warnings frozen once the compliance route is settled.
4. Manual text locked, then translated.
5. One proof, one approver, one signature.
6. Plates and print.

Steps three and four are the ones that slip, and both are yours to settle rather than the printer's.
