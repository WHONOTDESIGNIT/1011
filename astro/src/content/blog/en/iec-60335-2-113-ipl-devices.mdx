IEC 60335-2-113 is the particular-requirements standard that sits under IEC 60335-1 for cosmetic and beauty care appliances incorporating lasers and intense light sources. It exists because an IPL device is, legally and electrically, a household appliance that happens to emit a lot of light.

That framing decides everything downstream. The standard does not ask whether the device removes hair. It asks whether a person holding it can be hurt.

## Why IPL sits under a household-appliance standard at all

A home IPL device plugs into a wall socket, draws a few hundred watts, stores energy in a capacitor bank and discharges it through a xenon lamp. Strip away the beauty category and that description matches a lot of appliances already covered by IEC 60335-1.

The people writing the standard had two options. Treat the device as a medical device, which several markets do, or treat it as an appliance with an unusual light source. IEC 60335-2-113 takes the second path and then bolts on the optical hazard requirements that an ordinary appliance standard never needed.

If you want the wider picture of how different regulators place these devices, [the classification question is covered separately](/blog/how-regulators-classify-home-ipl). This piece stays on what the standard itself tests.

## The four risk families the standard tests

**Electric shock.** Insulation between mains and the user, leakage current, creepage and clearance distances. A device that flashes is also a device with switching transients, so the spacing rules are not academic.

**Temperature rise.** The housing, the window, the contact surface against skin. An IPL head that reaches a comfortable 30 °C in a lab at 23 °C can behave differently in a bathroom, and the standard tests the surface you touch, not the lamp inside.

**Mechanical strength.** Impact on the housing, stability, and what happens to the window when the device is dropped. A cracked sapphire window is an optical hazard, so this connects to the next family.

**Optical radiation.** The requirement that the device cannot be operated in a way that exposes the eye to the unfiltered flash. Interlocks, the contact sensor, and the time between flashes all feed into this.

Four families, one appliance. Most brands arrive at the lab expecting to discuss fluence and leave discussing creepage distances.

## What "abnormal operation" means for a device that flashes 999,999 times

Every IEC 60335 standard has an abnormal-operation section, and it is where designs get uncomfortable.

For an IPL device the interesting failures are the quiet ones. The cooling fan stops. The lamp fails to strike, so the capacitor bank stays charged and the control loop keeps trying. A component on the driver board fails short. The device is expected to fail safely in each case, which usually means no fire, no accessible live part, and no shrapnel.

This is where a cheap design shows itself. A fan failure that drives the lamp to thermal shutdown is a design decision. A fan failure that lets the housing soften is an omission.

## Which parts the sample build usually fails first

In our experience the first failures cluster in three places.

The temperature rise on the treatment window during a continuous session, because the test runs the device harder than a user would and the thermal path was designed for a normal session.

The creepage distance around the flash-trigger circuit, because the layout was copied from a lower-voltage design and nobody re-measured after the lamp voltage went up.

The interlock logic, because the contact sensor was tuned to detect skin reliably rather than to fail safe when it cannot.

None of these are exotic. All three are cheaper to fix on a bench than on a certification schedule.

## How the test sample differs from a production unit

The sample that goes to the lab is hand-built, usually with more thermal margin and tidier wiring than the line will produce. That is normal and the standard allows it.

The trap is what happens afterwards. The certificate describes the tested configuration. If production changes the capacitor bank supplier, the fan, or the housing material, the certificate does not automatically cover the change. A brand can end up holding a valid certificate for a device it no longer ships.

This is one of the reasons we tell brands to [put the seven documents in one place before paying a deposit](/blog/how-to-vet-ipl-device-manufacturer). A certificate with no change log tells you what was tested once, not what you are buying.

## The two documents people confuse

A supplier sends a certificate. It is one page, it carries a logo, and it says the model passed the standard. Most brands file it and move on.

The document that matters is the test report behind it. Forty pages, usually, and it says which clauses were applied, how many samples were destroyed, what the measured values were, and which deviations the lab accepted.

The difference matters for one practical reason. The certificate is a statement about a moment. The report is a record of a configuration. When you ask a question later, about a component swap or a new colour of housing, the report is the only document that can answer it.

We have seen brands hold a valid certificate for a device that no longer existed. The certificate was not forged. It was simply describing a build that had since changed, and nobody had re-issued anything because nothing had failed.

## What a test house needs before it can start

Four things, and each one can delay the booking on its own.

A slot. Test houses book weeks ahead, and the queue lengthens before a trade show.

Samples. Usually several units, because the abnormal-operation section destroys some of them. The samples must be the configuration you intend to ship, not a bench build with different internal spacing.

The technical file. Schematics, BOM, layout, thermal design notes, and the rating label artwork. A lab that has to ask for these after the sample arrives is a lab that has already started the clock.

The applicant's identity. The certificate names a party, and that party has to be a real legal entity with the authority to make the declaration. This is the same identity question that decides who the manufacturer of record is, and it is worth settling before the lab asks.

Get those four in place and the testing is a calendar problem. Get them wrong and it becomes a re-test problem, which costs both the slot and the schedule.

## What to ask your supplier for, in writing

Ask for the full test report, not the certificate cover page. The report shows the clauses tested and the measured values.

Ask which sample configuration was tested, and whether the current BOM matches it.

Ask what changed since the report was issued. A supplier who can answer this quickly has a change process. A supplier who cannot has a folder.

Ask how the device behaves when the lamp fails to strike. If the answer is a shrug, the abnormal-operation section was not taken seriously.

## The part most brands get wrong

They treat the safety certificate as a purchasing formality and the photobiological report as optional. The safety standard is the entry ticket. What it does not do is tell you whether the light coming out of the device is safe to look at, which is a different standard and a different failure mode.
