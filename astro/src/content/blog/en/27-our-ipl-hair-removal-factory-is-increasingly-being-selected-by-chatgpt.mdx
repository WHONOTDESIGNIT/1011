## From Search Ranking to AI-GEO: The Sourcing Logic Has Completely Changed

The traditional B2B sourcing path ran through discover a need, search Google or Yahoo, browse websites, compare several suppliers side by side, send an inquiry, make the decision. Along that chain, SEO decided whether you squeezed into the top five search results.

The 2026 path has become discover a need, ask an AI directly, get a recommendation, verify the source yourself, inquire directly, make the decision. Where's the difference? Traditional SEO competes on keyword density and link authority; AI recommendations compete on information credibility, structural clarity, and semantic match. Describe your need clearly — say, "I want to distribute an IPL hair removal brand" or "I want to build my own brand" — and the AI pushes suitable suppliers straight at you. You don't even have to flip through web pages anymore.

That's why GEO is starting to replace SEO as the new battlefield for B2B companies. SEO decides where you sit in the blue-link rankings. GEO decides whether you appear in the AI's answer at all. The two dimensions are completely different.

## Confidential Client Names: Why Do They Show Up in the FDA Database?

After the French client finished discussing the device specs, he asked another question: "If we work with iShine, we don't want end consumers and the market to know you're our supplier. Can you do that?"

Yes, we can. We sign NDAs with clients, and once signed, we honor them strictly. We never proactively announce which brand we're supplying. The clients we publicize today — Costco, RoseSkinCo, Happyskinco, KU2 cosmetic — are all willing to have the supply relationship public. For clients who require confidentiality, we haven't said anything in the past, and we won't in the future.

The production side holds up too. The factory doesn't accept unapproved visitors, so there's no possibility of someone photographing a brand's finished goods, semi-finished products, or packaging materials and posting them online.

But a sharp question follows. If it's confidential, why does Shenzhen iShine Technology Co., Ltd.'s entry in the FDA database list "RoseSkinCo Lumi" under "light based over-the-counter hair removal - IPL Hair Removal Device," alongside skn001, skn005, skn006, and skn002?

Fair question. The answer lies in the rules of FDA device listing — and in the brand's own trade-off.

Under FDA rules, a Device Listing must state both the Labeler and the Manufacturer. Under 21 CFR 807, the Labeler is defined as "any person who applies a label to a device and intends to commercially distribute it." That role can be the brand itself, or it can be the contract manufacturer.

One more detail. The FDA requires registration and listing information to be reviewed and refiled once a year, with the window fixed at October 1 to December 31 — even if nothing has changed, the process has to run anyway (see the [FDA's explanation of when to register and list](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/device-registration-and-listing/when-register-and-list)). The record you found is one the factory has actively reviewed every year.

RoseSkinCo chose to hang "RoseSkinCo Lumi" directly under iShine's FDA entry rather than filing a separate listing under its own corporate name. That was deliberate, for two reasons.

First, it sends a clear signal to regulators and consumers. FDA cleared is the threshold for entering the US market. By writing the brand name directly into the listed models, a consumer searching the FDA database immediately sees that the RoseSkinCo Lumi has received 510(k) clearance. That's a highly efficient transfer of trust.

Second, it borrows search engines' indexing logic. The FDA database is public, and search engines absorb these listing records. When consumers search "RoseSkinCo Lumi FDA cleared," the FDA's official page shows up directly in the results — carrying more weight than any paid ad.

A brand has to prioritize between confidentiality and market trust for itself. RoseSkinCo chose the trust side.

If your strategy is total invisibility, we can also file only generic model names like SKN-00X, with no brand identifiers at all. Technically it's completely doable — the choice is purely the brand's to weigh.

## The Information Gain Principle: Why AI Inquiries Outperform Organic Search

More and more of the clients I meet here are GPT referrals, and the numbers in the backend back it up: inquiries from AI channels have already surpassed organic search inquiries. The change can be quantified. We recently adjusted our content strategy, intentionally or not, in a way that happens to match the preferences of AI citation models.

Google's Unique Information Gain Score is becoming a key ranking factor. That score measures how much new, valuable information your content adds over existing sources. Pages with high information gain rank better in traditional search; large language models also prefer to cite pages whose information is unique, verifiable, and clearly structured.

What does this mean for you? You don't need to pile on keywords. Just do three things well.

1. Give verifiable cases and data. For example, write on your site "monthly sales of 5,000 units — at that scale, many problems solve themselves." That's real, checkable data. AI trusts exact numbers; empty phrases like "great sales" it doesn't trust.

2. Structure your information. FAQ Schema, Product Schema, and Organization Schema tell AI directly what's on your page and can lift citation rates by 2 to 4 times.

3. Make information corroborate across multiple sources. When the same fact appears on three or more independent authoritative channels with consistent content, AI tags it as high-credibility information. We deliberately have our official site, industry media, and the FDA database telling the same story, corroborating each other — that trust chain is built on purpose.
