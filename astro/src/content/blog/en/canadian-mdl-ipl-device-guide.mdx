The short answer: no. For an at-home IPL hair removal device, a Canadian Medical Device Licence is almost never worth the money.

We're a factory that manufactures these devices. We've run the numbers. We've watched sellers list on Amazon.ca for years without an MDL. And we tell our own OEM clients to skip it.

Here's the licence data that backs that up — pulled straight from Health Canada's own database.

## What a Health Canada Licence Search Actually Returns

Search the [Medical Devices Active Licence Listing](https://health-products.canada.ca/mdall-limh/?lang=eng) for "IPL hair removal device" and the result is almost empty.

Six device names. That's the whole list.

Five of the visible entries split across three companies:

- **Shenzhen Ulike Smart Electronics Co., Ltd.** — the Shenzhen OEM factory behind the Ulike brand. Three licences: 111841, 111844, and 110474.
- **Ulike International Industrial Pte. Ltd.** — Ulike's Singapore entity. Licence 114731.
- **Blue Echo Care LLC** — a company out of Portland, Oregon. Licence 114125.

Read that again. The entire pool of MDL holders for at-home IPL in Canada boils down to essentially one manufacturer (Ulike, through two of its entities) and one US company. Not sixty. Not six hundred.

Six.

And under [Health Canada's import rules](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/importation-exportation/commercial-use-health-products-guidance.html), you need a licence to bring one of these into the country for sale at all. So the gap isn't a technicality.

## The Gap That Doesn't Add Up

Now go look at Amazon.ca. Type "IPL hair removal device" into the search bar.

Hundreds of listings. Dozens of brands you've never heard of. Row after row of devices with the same generic plastic housing, the same spec sheet, the same "FDA / CE / MDL approved" badge in the corner of the product image.

That gap is the entire story. Six real licences on one side. A flooded marketplace on the other.

Every one of those sellers claims to be compliant. And mathematically, most of them can't be — unless a lot of them are sharing the same six licences, which the Health Canada record does not support.

## How Those Listings Get Past Amazon Canada's Review

We've watched this happen for years, so let me explain the two ways sellers fake their way onto Amazon.ca.

**One: forged documents.** Fake MDL certificates. Fake sales contracts. A fake stamp from an MDL-approved manufacturer. The seller downloads a licence number from somewhere, pastes it into a template, and submits it during the listing review. The reviewer on the other end checks a box and moves on.

**Two: expired licences that still pass.** Amazon's audit system doesn't always check the expiry date on an MDL. A device that held a valid licence five years ago, and let it lapse, still gets treated as current. The seller trades on a number that's dead.

Don't take our word on the second one. Amazon's own seller forum is full of it — threads titled "[Medical Device Policy Lack Verification System](https://sellercentral.amazon.ca/seller-forums/discussions/t/9f7fbc73-87f0-4d38-bdf6-ccb18f1b317c)" and "[Action required for your listings of Class II Medical devices](https://sellercentral.amazon.ca/seller-forums/discussions/t/718c8d4b-1fce-4e5c-8369-f348bee7f8f1)" show sellers caught between Amazon's on-paper requirements and its off-paper enforcement. The rules say you need an MDL for a Class II device. The review system doesn't always actually check whether the one you submitted is real, current, or yours.

That second gap matters more than people think. Health Canada licences expire. A number from 2019 is not a licence in 2026. But if nobody verifies the date, it might as well be.

## Should You Buy an MDL? The Math From a Factory Floor

This is the question brands actually ask us. Not "is MDL certification real" — of course it's real. The question is whether it's a smart business decision.

Our first-party answer, from shipping these devices ourselves: no, it is not.

Three reasons.

**The market is tiny.** Canada's population is smaller than California's. The number of people who will spend money on an at-home IPL device — versus a salon session or a drugstore razor — is a fraction of that. You are fighting over a very small pie.

**The certification costs $16,000 to $20,000 USD.** That's the range for an MDL on an at-home IPL device, and it's not a one-time expense — there's ongoing compliance, labelling, and renewal overhead on top of the upfront fee.

**You'd be competing against fake players.** Even if you do everything by the book, your honest listing sits next to fifty sellers who forged their documents and got through. Your $18,000 bought you a licence that a competitor faked in an afternoon with a PDF editor.

So the return just isn't there. A tiny market, a five-figure cost, and a playing field that's already tilted against the honest ones.

## The $16,000 to $20,000 Question

Let me put a sharper point on the cost.

If the Canadian market could absorb enough volume to pay back $18,000 inside a year, the math changes. It can't, for most brands. You'd need a real distribution channel in Canada — retail, not just Amazon — to justify it.

And here's the thing nobody talks about: the sellers running fake licences have already priced their devices as if MDL costs nothing. Because for them, it did. So your honest listing is more expensive to bring to market, more expensive to maintain, and competes against people whose entire compliance department is a cracked version of Adobe.

That's not a market. That's a trap.

## What Happens When Health Canada and Amazon Wake Up

We're not going to sugarcoat this for the fakers.

The crackdown is coming. It always does, and it usually arrives all at once. A policy change, a single well-publicized enforcement action, one consumer complaint that lands in the wrong inbox — and suddenly the review team starts checking expiry dates and calling manufacturers to verify authorization letters.

When that happens, the fake listings go down in a week. The sellers who built their business on forged documents lose their accounts, their inventory, and their FBA balances.

We've seen this play out in other categories before, so we wish them luck. They'll need it.

## What We Recommend Instead

If you're a brand thinking about Canada, here's what we tell our clients:

Skip the MDL as your entry strategy. Canada is not where you launch an at-home IPL device in 2026. Put that $18,000 toward a market that actually pays it back — the US, the UK, or the EU, where the volume justifies real compliance work.

And if you've already decided Canada is part of your roadmap, and you want to do it properly — with a real licence, not a forged one — that's a different conversation. Real compliance in Canada means both an [MDL for the device and an MDEL for the establishment](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/establishment-licences/medical-devices-establishment-licensing.html), and it means the [import paperwork to match](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/drugs-health-products/compliance-enforcement/importation-exportation/commercial-use-health-products-guidance.html).

We keep an open door on this. We've supported brands through MDL certification before, and we'll do it again for the ones who are serious about the Canadian market. If there's a real business case, we'll help you build it the legitimate way — real licence, real authorization, no PDF editing.
