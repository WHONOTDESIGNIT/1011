**Short answer:** answer with three questions about her skin, her calendar and her history, then recommend maintenance at home only if all three fit. The clients you decline remember it, and they come back for the sessions that actually need a professional.

## The question behind the question

She asks "can I do this at home" while she is on your table. What she means is one of three things, and they need different answers.

She might want to stop coming. She might want to stretch the time between bookings. Or she might have seen a device on sale and wants permission to buy it.

Only the second is a home device conversation. The first is a pricing conversation about your own course. The third is a specification conversation, and most spas get it wrong by answering the price.

## Three questions, in this order

| Ask | Why it decides the answer |
|---|---|
| Have you used a home device before? | A first-time user needs a schedule she can keep. Price comes second. A repeat user is asking whether yours is better than hers. |
| What medication are you on? | Some photosensitising drugs put her outside what a home device should be used with. A negative answer ends the conversation before price or suitability come up. |
| Which areas? | Face, bikini and underarm each have their own boundaries, and a device bought for legs may not be indicated for the others. |

The medication question is the one to ask out loud, every time. It is the difference between a recommendation and an assumption.

## What the recommendation sounds like

Something close to this, in your own words: "Use it between appointments on the areas we have already worked on. Keep the schedule for eight weeks, then come in and let us look."

Then name the exception: what she must not do in the 48 hours before or after a session, and when to stop and call you.

Notice what the recommendation does not contain: a claim about permanence, a number of sessions, or a promise that she can skip the course.

## What the device does not change

Your treatment still does the part a handset cannot: the first sessions, the areas she cannot reach evenly by herself, and the skin that needs a professional's judgement.

The retail margin on a device is smaller than your treatment margin. The value is in the client who keeps her booking because the maintenance is no longer the reason she quits.

## What you will have to justify to the owner

The question is whether a spa should be in the business of recommending hardware at all. The answer rests on three things you can measure.

Referral conversion: of the clients you told about maintenance at home, how many booked again within twelve weeks. Compare with clients you did not tell.

Complaint rate: recommendations that ended in irritation or a refund. A spa that recommends badly loses more than it gains, which is why the exclusions matter more than the sales script.

Retail attachment: units sold per hundred clients seen. Small numbers are fine here. A spa selling two units a month with a ninety percent rebooking rate is doing better than one selling twenty with returns.

## The sentence to use in the meeting

"If we recommend the wrong thing to the wrong client, we own the complaint. If we recommend nothing, she buys from a marketplace and we hear about it six weeks later anyway."

That is the honest version, and it usually settles the question. The spa is already in the conversation. The only decision is whether it is in it with a protocol or without one.

## What the first order should contain

One unit for the treatment team to use on each other, one for the retail shelf, and one manual per staff member who will recommend it.

Check the manual before the training. If it does not answer the medication question, no amount of briefing will make the recommendation safe. Ask the supplier for the contraindication list in your language and read it yourself.

Then agree who says no. A spa where three people can recommend and nobody can refuse will recommend to the wrong client within a month.
