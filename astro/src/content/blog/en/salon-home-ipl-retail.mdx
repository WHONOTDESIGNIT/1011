**Short answer:** it doesn't compete if you sell it as maintenance between appointments. It competes when the device is priced near a course of treatments, or when nobody explains the difference at the counter. Those two failures are fixable before you order stock.

## The two calendars a client has

Your client has a treatment calendar and a home calendar. The treatment room owns the first: the session she books, pays for, and cannot do herself. The home calendar is everything between sessions, and until now you have been handing it to a razor.

A home IPL device belongs in the second calendar. Not because it replaces your treatment, but because the maintenance window is where clients lose the habit, and a client who loses the habit stops booking.

Three salons we work with describe the same pattern: clients who buy a device for maintenance come back for the session they cannot do at home. Clients who buy nothing drift.

## The three numbers to check before you stock one

| Number | Why it decides the sale |
|---|---|
| Price vs a course of treatments | If the device costs the same as four sessions, it reads as a replacement. Price it clearly below a course, or bundle it with one. |
| Margin per unit after returns | A device sold with no explanation comes back. Budget a return rate before you budget the profit. |
| Staff time per sale | Fifteen minutes of explanation at the counter is the difference between a sale and a refund. If nobody has the fifteen minutes, do not stock it. |

## What the counter has to be able to say

The staff who already explain aftercare are the ones who can sell this. They know the client's skin type, they know what she has had done, and they know which questions mean she should not buy at all.

Write the answers down. Two sentences each, in the language your staff actually speaks:

- What the device does that the treatment does not.
- How often she uses it, in weeks, not "as needed".
- What she must not do in the days around a session.
- When to stop and call you instead.

That last one protects the sale more than any claim on the box.

## Where the money actually is

Retail margin on a device is thinner than treatment margin. On a single unit it will not look like much next to a course booking.

The return comes from the client who stays in the habit. A client who maintains at home is a client still booking at month six. That is the number to watch, not the first month's retail revenue.

## What you will have to justify to the owner

You will be asked one question: does this sell treatments or steal them. Answer with three numbers rather than an opinion.

The first is the retail margin per unit after returns. If it is thinner than a treatment, say so before someone else works it out.

The second is the return rate you are budgeting. A device sold with fifteen minutes of explanation and a written schedule comes back less often than one sold off a shelf. Put a number on the difference and you own the argument.

The third is the rebooking rate of device buyers against non-buyers at month six. That is the number the owner actually cares about, because it answers whether maintenance keeps clients or releases them.

Bring those three and the conversation is about stock levels. Bring none and it becomes a debate about whether devices are a distraction, which is a debate you lose on price alone.

## The objection you will hear

"we already sell treatments" is not an objection to the device. It is a statement about the treatment room, and the answer is to agree with it. The device does not go in the treatment room. It goes home with the client.

Say that in one sentence, then hand over the schedule. Salons that frame the device as a take-home continuation sell it. Salons that frame it as cheaper equipment argue about the machine instead.

## The first order, and what to check when it lands

Two units and a display. That is enough to test whether the conversation happens, and small enough that a slow start costs shelf space rather than cash.

When the carton arrives, check three things before it goes behind the counter. The manual is in the language your staff speak. The plug matches your country's socket, not the factory's. And the box carries your market's warnings rather than a generic set.

That third check is the one salons skip, and it is the one a customer reads on the way home.
