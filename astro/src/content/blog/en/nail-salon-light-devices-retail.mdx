**Short answer:** the client who comes back every three weeks already trusts your judgment. Put one or two home IPL devices on the shelf, introduce them when she asks, and when she says nothing, let a conversation about hair or skin open the door. The rest of this article is about what your staff has to be able to say once that door is open.

## Mia, every three weeks

Mia books a set every three weeks and has done it for two years. She sits down, puts her hands on the table, and talks: the wedding she is going to, her sister, the patch of dry skin on her cheek that would not settle.

Somewhere in those two years she stopped asking what things cost and started asking what you recommend. When your technician said the strengthening base coat would help the peeling, she bought it without checking the price. When someone suggested a different shape for her thumb, she trusted it.

That is what two years of a repeated service buys a salon. A stranger reading reviews has nothing like it.

## What the third year makes possible

The same client, in the same chair, mentions the hair on her upper lip. She has thought about laser, priced it once, and stopped at the number. She asks whether the device on the shelf would work for her.

You already know the answer to the question underneath it. She wants to know whether you think she should spend this money on herself. Two years of being right about base coats and nail shapes is the reason the answer lands.

A marketplace listing reaches a person who has never heard your voice. This conversation reaches someone who has been listening for two years.

## One or two devices, and the room stays the room

A nail salon that fills a wall with devices turns into a shop with chairs. The version that works keeps the counter, keeps the appointment, and sets out one or two devices where a client can pick them up.

Two is a reasonable ceiling for a first year. One for the team to use, one to sell. When the second one leaves the shop, reorder it and mention it to the client who bought the first.

## When she asks

The question usually comes in one of three shapes: does this work, does it hurt, and how often. Thirty seconds each, in your own words.

*Does it work?* It slows regrowth over weeks, and it keeps what a course of professional treatment has already done. It treats a small area at a time.

*Does it hurt?* Warm, like a rubber band flicking. It goes down on the lower settings.

*How often?* Once a week on most areas, and it asks for a rest if the skin stays pink.

If the technician has to reach for the box to answer those three, the shelf waits. Training for this takes one page and a handset that the staff have held themselves.

## When she says nothing

Most clients never ask. They do not know the device is for sale, or they assume it costs more than they would spend.

Two topics open the door without a pitch. The first is the hair a client is already fighting: the upper lip, the chin, the neck, the arm. The second is the skin she is unhappy about: ingrown hairs, darker patches from shaving, the irritation under her jaw.

Both come up naturally while her hands are occupied and she is talking anyway. A technician who names the device once, in the middle of that conversation, has done the work. A technician who brings it up every appointment for six months has not.

## The two devices in the room are not the same light

Your client already associates you with a device that shines light on a hand. The difference is what the light reaches, and it is worth saying out loud once.

| The gel lamp | The home IPL device |
|---|---|
| The light cures a coating on the nail | The light puts energy into skin |
| Too long wastes a set | Too much irritates skin |
| No contraindications | Medication, skin condition, recent treatments |
| A mistake costs a manicure | A mistake costs a client |

## Three clients to turn away

- Anyone on a photosensitising medication, whatever the dose.
- Anyone with an active skin infection or broken skin on the area.
- Anyone who had a wax or epilation on that area in the last two weeks.

Say it plainly, once, and hand over [the full contraindication list](/blog/33-who-should-not-use-ipl-hair-removal-contraindications). Then let the client think it over. The clients who are turned away are the ones who send their friends.

## What the owner needs before the first sale

Four things, and one of them is a formality that stops being a formality after a complaint.

**A one-page script.** The three exclusions, the three answers, and the sentence that says come back in eight weeks. A course gets postponed; a page gets learnt.

**The insurance question.** Some policies exclude advising anyone about skin. Find out which one you hold before the first device leaves the shelf.

**The return rate.** A device sold without the exclusions comes back, and a nail set's margin cannot absorb that. The first quarter tells you whether the script is working.

**The first order.** One unit for the staff, two for sale. [The private label terms](/services/private-label#commercial-terms-section) state the minimum order, the price band and the tooling rules; the platforms themselves are on [the products page](/products).

The pedicure corner, where a conversation about legs already happens and the client's hands are free.

Next to the polish wall it reads as something to add to the basket, and basket items in a service business are the ones that come back. The placement is doing sales work before anyone says a word.

## What two years of trust is worth

Count the clients who have been with you for more than a year. Each one is a person who already takes your word on a base coat.

Mia bought a device because a technician she trusts named it once. The shelf does not need a campaign. It needs a technician who has held the handset, one page of script, and one or two devices within reach.
