**Short answer:** the hair salon sells a device. The room, the training and the reason to own a professional machine all stay outside the door, and the client relationship plus the conversation are what a device sale needs.

## The conversation already happens

A client sits in your chair for forty minutes. Somewhere in that time she mentions the hair on her upper lip, or the ingrown hairs on her neck, or that she is tired of shaving her arms.

Today that conversation ends with advice. It could end with a product, if the advice is specific enough.

## What a hair salon can say that a marketplace cannot

Three things, and every one of them concerns the client:

| What you can say | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| "This is for maintenance between appointments" | Sets the expectation that stops the return |
| "Not on the area we just coloured" | A local contraindication a marketplace cannot see |
| "Come back in eight weeks and let me look" | Turns a sale into a second visit |

That middle line is your unfair advantage. A marketplace page cannot see a fresh colour treatment or an irritated scalp.

## The trap: selling it like a brush

Devices sold as impulse items come back. The pattern is consistent enough that we build the return rate into how we brief salon stockists: if nobody spends fifteen minutes explaining the schedule and the exclusions, the unit is a refund with extra steps.

Two fixes cost nothing. Put the device behind the counter. And make the person who explains aftercare the person who sells it.

## Which clients to say no to

Say no to anyone with an active skin infection, a fresh colour or chemical service on the area, or a medication that makes the skin photosensitive. Say no, and say why, in one sentence.

A salon that says no three times a week sells more devices over a year, because the four clients who were told the truth repeat it.

## The stock arithmetic

Start with a number you can explain. Two units behind the counter and one on display is a test. Twenty units is a purchase order for a product nobody has asked for yet.

Your device supplier should be able to sell below that first number. Ours can: [the private label page](/services/private-label#commercial-terms-section) states the MOQ and the band it sits in.

## What you will have to justify to the owner

Stocking a device in a hair salon invites one question: why this and not more colour retail.

The answer sits in the client conversation. Count how often a client raises hair removal in a week. Most salons underestimate it until someone writes it down for a fortnight.

Then count how many of those clients bought anything because of the conversation. That gap is the opportunity, and your own chair measures it. A supplier's deck measures nothing of the kind.

## The three numbers to bring

Conversations per week: the raw demand signal.

Attachment rate: units sold per hundred conversations. Count the conversations, and the client count stops flattering you.

Return rate: the number that decides whether the shelf stays.

A salon doing four conversations a week and selling one unit a fortnight is a salon with a working add-on. A salon selling ten a month and taking four back has a training problem to solve. The product is already doing its job, and the briefing is where the work goes.

## The risk to name out loud

If nobody on the floor can explain the schedule, the device turns into refund work and the staff stop offering it. Say that in the meeting before it happens. It is the difference between a proposal and a complaint six weeks later.

## The first order, in practical terms

One unit for the team, two for sale. Ask for a manual in your language and a counter card that states the three cases where you say no.

Then decide the departure point: does the device live behind the counter or on a shelf. Behind the counter, with the person who explains aftercare holding it, is the version that survives its first quarter.

Check the plug on arrival. Salons have burnt a week of goodwill on a device that arrived with the factory's plug.
