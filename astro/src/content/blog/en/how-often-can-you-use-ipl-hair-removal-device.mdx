## The short answer

**Twice a week for 6 weeks, or once a week for 12 weeks. Both are in the manual, and either one works.** The first is faster because it fits more sessions into the same six weeks. What is not in the manual is every day, or three times a week, because neither leaves the 3 to 4 days of recovery that a treated area needs between sessions.

| Phase | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Quick start | **Twice a week for 6 weeks** | Wait 3 to 4 days between treatments on the same area |
| Busy schedule, or sensitive skin | **Once a week for 12 weeks** | Same total number of sessions, spread further apart |
| Maintenance, months 1 to 3 | Every **2 to 3 weeks** | Once the course is finished |
| Maintenance, month 4 onward | Every **3 to 4 weeks** | |
| Month 9 onward | As needed for touch-ups | Treat when you see regrowth |

The simple rule underneath all of it: treat when you see regrowth. Almost no hair means wait another week or two. Light fuzz or noticeable regrowth means treat now.

That spacing is not ours alone. The American Academy of Dermatology describes light-based hair removal in a clinic the same way, as a series of sessions rather than one, with results accumulating across them ([AAD, laser hair removal FAQs](https://www.aad.org/public/cosmetic/hair-removal/laser-hair-removal-faqs)).

## Can you use it every day?

No.

A flash puts heat into the follicle and into the epidermis sitting over it. That heat does not clear in an hour. Day one you get the redness you expect. Day two the skin is still warm and you flash it again. By day four the cumulative dose has outrun the skin's ability to repair, and what you have is a burn, not a faster result.

There is a regulatory reason the instructions carry so much weight here. A home IPL is expected to be operated by a layperson with no supervision, and the FDA's design guidance for home-use devices is built around exactly that problem, telling manufacturers to design for the mistakes ordinary users make ([FDA, Design Considerations for Devices Intended for Home Use](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/design-considerations-devices-intended-for-home-use)). Frequency is one of the mistakes that has to be handled in the instruction manual, because no sensor can stop you from picking the device up again tomorrow.

## Twice a week, or once a week?

Pick by lifestyle and by how your skin answers, not by which one sounds more serious.

Twice a week finishes the course in 6 weeks. That is the schedule our manual lists first, and the reason it works is arithmetic: two sessions need one gap, and 3 to 4 days fits inside seven. **Three times a week does not work, and the arithmetic is why.** Three sessions need two gaps of 3 to 4 days, and there is no version of a seven-day week where that fits. If you have seen a three-times-a-week schedule somewhere, it did not come from a device manual.

Once a week for 12 weeks delivers the same number of sessions at half the frequency. It suits sensitive skin, and it suits anyone whose week is unpredictable enough that a midweek session would get skipped. The total dose over the course is what produces the result; how you spread it is a scheduling decision.

One thing to know before you plan around either: **the interval is per area, not per person.** Legs on Sunday and underarms on Wednesday is fine, and it is how most people actually get through a full-body course without quitting in the first month. Trying to treat everything in one sitting is what makes the twice-a-week schedule feel impossible.

## What your device manual is actually specifying

That frequency line is not a marketing figure. It is a duty-cycle limit.

Every flash heats the lamp and the optical stack along with the skin, and the manual's interval is the spacing the device was tested at. In the EU, light-emitting beauty-care appliances are assessed against IEC 60335-2-113, the particular standard covering this product category and what its instructions must tell the user ([IEC 60335-2-113](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/110222)). A manufacturer telling you to flash the same area daily is either delivering very little energy per pulse, or has not tested the duty cycle and is guessing.

So when a manual says twice a week with 3 to 4 days between, read it as the tested number rather than a cautious suggestion.

## How to choose the intensity

Do a patch test at level 1 at least 24 hours before your first full treatment. Then start the course at level 1 to 3, not at the top.

Passing looks like this: mild redness that fades within a few hours, warmth that disappears quickly, and small bumps at the follicle openings that clear on their own in a day or two. When you get that, hold the same level for a second session before you move up. Raise one level at a time, and only after several well-tolerated sessions at the level below.

Failing looks like this: burning pain, blistering or crusting, swelling that worsens over hours, or darkening that deepens over days. Any one of those means stop, drop the level, or leave that patch alone entirely. Our own manual draws the line at 48 hours: if discomfort or redness is still there two days later, stop increasing the level.

Now the part that decides whether the whole course works. **The most common reason someone finishes twelve weeks and sees nothing is that they never went above level two.** Under-powering is a quieter failure than over-powering, and it costs you the entire course. The intensity you want is the highest one your skin tolerates without a reaction, and the patch test is how you find it instead of guessing.

That is also where hardware stops being a spec-sheet detail. Contact cooling and skin-tone sensing exist so the tolerable level is higher. A sapphire window held near 10 °C against the skin changes how much energy you can sit through, which matters more on the twice-a-week schedule than the weekly one, because there is less time between sessions for the skin to settle.

## Skin tone sets the ceiling

Fitzpatrick type decides where you start, and it does not negotiate.

| Skin type | Where to start | How fast to ramp |
|---|---|---|
| I to II | Level 1 to 3 is usually tolerable | Faster, but still one level at a time |
| III to IV | Level 1 to 3 | Standard ramp |
| V | Lowest level only, after a patch test | Slow, and stop at the first sign of pigment change |
| VI | Not suitable for IPL | — |

Fair skin is the group with the most headroom, which is exactly why fair skin is also the group most likely to over-do it. There is no reaction stopping you at level three, so nothing tells you that level four was a mistake until you wake up with a mark. Ramp on patience, not on the absence of pain.

## The lamp math nobody puts in the manual

Our SKN011 platform carries a quartz lamp rated for **999,999 flashes**, and the manual describes that as a lifetime supply, good for 30 or more years of typical use. The same manual times a **full-body session at about 10 minutes in Glide mode**, with roughly two seconds between flashes at the top level.

Do the division and the conclusion is not close. You are not going to run out of flashes, and the lamp is not the thing that decides whether your course works. Almost nobody who buys a home IPL and gets a result does it by using the device harder. They get there by still using it in month four.

## What we would tell you if you only read one paragraph

The interval is the treatment. Twice a week for six weeks or once a week for twelve, with 3 to 4 days between sessions on the same area either way, then back off to every few weeks. Run a patch test at level 1, start the course at level 1 to 3, and raise it only while your skin stays quiet. Skip a session if life gets in the way, and do not pay it back. The people who get a result from a home IPL are the ones still using it in month four, and no energy spec on any box changes that.
