**Short answer:** a quality agreement decides what happens when a shipment is wrong. Most private-label IPL projects do not have one, and when a claim arrives the argument falls back to the purchase order and to goodwill. Eight clauses carry the weight, and two of them decide whether a defect can ever be traced to a batch.

## Why the purchase order is not enough

A PO states what to make and what to pay. It rarely states what counts as a defect, who inspects, at what level, what happens if the level is missed, and how a change to the product gets communicated.

That silence is survivable while orders are small. It becomes expensive at the first container where somebody finds a problem, because by then the two sides are negotiating from memory.

A quality agreement takes an afternoon and removes that argument. The clauses below are ordered by how much damage their absence causes.

## The eight clauses

| # | Clause | What it has to say | Cost of leaving it out |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Inspection standard | Which standard, which sampling level, who inspects, and where | "We inspected it" versus "not the way we inspect it" |
| 2 | Defect definitions | What counts as critical, major and minor, with examples for this product | Every scratch becomes a debate |
| 3 | Change notification | Who tells whom, how far in advance, and what a change to the bill of materials triggers | Buyer discovers a component change after the goods arrive |
| 4 | Retained samples | How many units are kept from each batch, for how long, stored where | A defect cannot be traced to a produced batch |
| 5 | Complaint response | Acknowledgement and root-cause timelines, and who owns the analysis | Claims sit unresolved for weeks |
| 6 | Recall cooperation | Who traces which units, who owns the cost, who notifies | Nobody knows which serials shipped where |
| 7 | Record retention | Which records, for how long, in what form | The evidence expires before the claim does |
| 8 | Audit right | Notice period, scope, and whether remote audits count | The buyer cannot verify anything independently |

Two of those rows deserve their own paragraphs.

## Clause 3, in practice

A bill of materials is a living document. Components become unavailable, a supplier offers a better price, a certification requires a substitution. A revision is normal.

What is not normal is the buyer finding out later. A change to the reflector cup, the fan, the adapter or the cell can alter performance, compliance scope and warranty exposure at once.

The clause should require four things:

- **Written notice before implementation**, with the effective date.
- **The reason**, in one line, because "component changed" tells the buyer nothing about risk.
- **The buyer's written acceptance** for anything that touches the electrical design, the cooling, the battery or a claimed parameter.
- **Notice and acceptance as the same document.** Screening items and artwork can move on notice alone; the parts above need a signature.

The mechanism that makes this work is mundane: one page, dated, filed with the revision, sent before production starts. Factories that do it well send a page; factories that do it badly send a phone call.

## Clause 4, and why samples decide arguments

Without retained samples, a defect discussion has no reference point. The factory says the unit left in specification; the buyer says it does not match what was approved. Both may be describing the same unit.

Three units per batch, kept for the commercial life of the model, sealed, with the batch code written on both the bag and the record, closes that gap. It also makes first-article approval mean something, because there is something physical to compare against two years later.

## How to test a supplier before signing

Three questions, asked before the first order, tell you whether the agreement will be honoured:

1. Can you show me the last three change notices for this platform, with dates?
2. Where are retained samples kept, and can I see the batch record for the last run?
3. If I book a remote audit with a week's notice, what will I be able to look at?

A factory with a working system answers all three from a folder. A factory with a documents-only system answers the first and struggles with the second.

## What the agreement is not

It is not a quality certificate, and it does not replace the inspection. A quality agreement governs the relationship; the audit and the inspection produce evidence.

It is also not a one-sided document. Clauses 1 to 4 protect the buyer. Clauses 5 to 7 protect the factory as much as the brand, because they define when a claim is valid and who pays for it. An agreement that only imposes obligations gets signed and ignored.

## What we do, and what we do not

We sign a quality agreement before the first production run, issue a written change notice for every bill-of-materials revision that affects a specified parameter, keep retained samples per batch, and open the file for audit. We do not sign an agreement we cannot operate, and we do not claim a defect level we have not measured.
