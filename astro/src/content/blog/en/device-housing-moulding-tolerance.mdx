**Short answer:** yes. The tight tolerances sit in a separate internal frame, and the formed shell only has to close over it. A handset with a lamp window and a battery bay asks blow moulding for internal features it does not produce directly. Four tolerances decide whether that arrangement works.

## The four tolerances

| Tolerance | What it controls | What fails without it |
|---|---|---|
| Shell to frame fit | How the two halves close | A rattle, a gap, a seam a client can feel |
| Lamp window seat | Alignment of the optical path | Light escaping the edge |
| Join strength under drop | Whether the unit survives a courier | A split seam, a dead device |
| Heat path | Where the lamp and driver heat goes | A shell that warps and discolours, and a thermal cut-out that trips |

The frame carries the tight tolerances. The shell can be formed to a wider one and still close.

## Thermoforming's fit

Short runs and lower tooling cost are what the process does well, along with fast changes to the outer shape. If a brand wants a distinctive silhouette in a short run, a formed shell over an internal frame is the cheaper way to start.

The shell is a thin wall over a structure. How the unit feels in the hand comes from the frame underneath it.

## Blow moulding's fit

Hollow bodies and bottles are where it earns its keep: a continuous wall is the point of the part. A handset with a window, a battery bay and a driver board needs internal features that blow moulding does not produce directly.

You can blow mould a shell and add inserts. Every insert is another assembly step.

## The question to ask before quoting

Get the internal frame drawing. It shows where the tolerances sit, whether the window is a separate part, and whether the driver mounts to the shell or to the frame.

The drop test the design has to pass decides how thick the wall has to be, so ask for that requirement in the same conversation.

## The two-part question

Expect the question: why two parts?

Bring the tooling cost difference, and bring the volume at which it stops mattering. The two-part approach costs less to tool and more per unit. A single moulded shell costs more to tool and less per unit, with less assembly work at the end.

Where the two lines cross depends on the tooling quote for the shape you want, so that quote is the one to carry into the meeting.

Bring the failure mode too. A formed shell that rattles comes back as a return.

## The question to ask the customer

Ask for the drop test. That requirement decides how thick the wall has to be, and without it there is nothing to grade the wall against.

## The RFQ pack

Send the internal frame drawing, the drop requirement and the volume estimate. Ask for a tolerance stack on the window seat, and for a quote that separates tooling from piece price.

A piece price on its own does not tell you whether the window seat will hold. The stack is where you find the tolerance the moulder will struggle with.

Ask what happens to the tooling if the project pauses. Ownership and storage are worth a line in the contract even when the answer looks obvious.

## FAQ

**Can thermoforming be used for an IPL device housing?**

Yes, when the design accepts a formed shell with a separate internal frame that holds the tight tolerances. Short runs and lower tooling cost are what the process does well.

**What are the four tolerances that decide a formed housing?**

Shell-to-frame fit, the lamp window seat, join strength under drop, and the heat path. Miss one and you get a rattle at the seam, light at the window edge, a split in transit, or a shell that warps as the lamp and driver heat builds up.

**Does blow moulding work for an IPL handset?**

Hollow bodies and bottles. A handset needs a lamp window, a battery bay and a driver board, and those need internal features blow moulding does not produce directly.

**At what volume does a two-part housing beat a single moulded shell?**

There is no fixed number. The two-part approach costs less to tool and more per unit. A single moulded shell costs more to tool, less per unit, and takes less assembly time. The crossover comes out of the tooling quote for the shape you want.
