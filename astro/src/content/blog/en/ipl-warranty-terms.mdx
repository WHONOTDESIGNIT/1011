**Short answer:** our standard warranty is one year from delivery. Three years costs five US dollars per unit, five years costs ten. The extension is a priced option, and it has to appear on the quotation and the proforma invoice, not only in the catalogue.

## One year, and a ladder above it

A single term for every platform is easier to administer than a term that varies by model, and easier for a brand to explain to its own customers. Ours is one year as standard.

| Cover | What it adds | Cost to the buyer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | Standard term | Included in the unit price |
| 3 years | Two additional years | +5 USD per unit |
| 5 years | Four additional years | +10 USD per unit |

Two things about that table matter more than the numbers.

The first is that the extension is priced **per unit**, because the exposure is per unit. A brand ordering five hundred devices and a brand ordering fifty thousand are buying the same term but not the same liability.

The second is that the ladder applies to **the same exclusion list** as the base term. An extension that quietly widens what is covered is not an extension; it is a different product with a different price.

## Why the extension belongs on the quote, not just the catalogue

A catalogue states what is available. A quotation states what is being sold. If the extension is only in the catalogue, the buyer discovers it after the price has been agreed, and the conversation becomes a negotiation about something that should have been a line item.

So the quotation carries three lines: the base term, the optional extension with its per-unit price, and the exclusion list by reference. The proforma invoice then repeats whichever option the buyer chose.

That is also the difference between a claim and an argument later. A PI that says "5 years, +10/unit" settles in one email. A PI that says nothing and a catalogue that says five years settles after three exchanges.

## A brand can sell the extension as its own SKU

The extension is a normal retail product once the brand owns the customer relationship. Add a separate SKU in the brand's own store, priced above what the extension costs at the factory, and the coverage becomes a margin line rather than a cost.

For a five-year extension costing ten dollars per unit at the factory, a retail uplift in the region of twenty-five dollars per unit is a reasonable starting point: it is visible enough to be worth buying, and it is priced against the claim cost rather than against nothing. The brand sets the final number, because the brand carries the consumer relationship and the local consumer law behind it.

Two cautions on that. In the EU and UK the commercial guarantee is in addition to statutory rights and cannot replace them, so the wording has to make that clear. And in some markets warranty terms are themselves regulated as unfair-contract terms if they are shorter than the law allows, which is worth checking before the SKU goes live.

## Four documents, one term

| Document | What it should state | What happens when it is vague |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogue or spec sheet | The base term, and that an extension exists | The buyer assumes the longest version they saw |
| Quotation | Base term, extension price, exclusions by reference | Price is negotiated, term is assumed |
| Proforma invoice | The option actually purchased | No contractual hook, so disputes become goodwill |
| User manual | The term and the exclusion list, in the market's language | The end customer reads a different story from the buyer |

## What "covered" has to mean

A warranty without a stated scope is a promise to argue. Four things belong in the scope.

**The defect class.** Material and workmanship defects under normal use.

**The exclusion list.** Misuse, unauthorised repair, water ingress beyond the stated rating, use outside the rated supply, and force majeure. One line each, and each checkable from the returned unit.

**Who pays freight.** A warranty that covers the part but not the return freight is half a warranty, and it is the half buyers care about. State both directions.

**What remedy applies.** Repair, replace the unit, or replace the part. Different costs, different lead times, and a supplier who leaves it open will choose the cheapest.

## Spare parts decide the years after the term

The lamp cartridge, the adapter, the cooling assembly and the housing set are what make a two-year-old unit repairable rather than scrap. Three questions before the first order: which parts can be supplied separately, for how long after the last production run, and can a whole-unit replacement be arranged for a unit that cannot be repaired in the field.

The middle question is the one nobody asks. A brand with ten thousand units in the field will need parts in year three for a model the factory stopped making.

## The pattern that damages accounts

A quotation stated a two-year warranty. A later reply, after other terms had been renegotiated, moved to one year without flagging the change. The buyer had asked about the warranty explicitly and recorded the answer. The correction never came, and the discrepancy sat in the thread until they raised it.

From the supplier's side it looked like a term adjustment. From the buyer's side it looked like a supplier whose numbers move, and every later correction in that thread was read through that lens.

The rule that came out of it: whenever a term changes after a quotation has gone out, the change gets a sentence of its own, in the same message, before anything else is discussed. It costs one line and protects everything else.

## What we do, and what we do not

We state one year as the standard term on the quotation and again on the proforma invoice, quote the three-year and five-year extensions per unit, include the exclusion list and the freight responsibility, and list which parts can be supplied separately. We do not extend cover without pricing it, and we do not let a term change quietly between documents.
