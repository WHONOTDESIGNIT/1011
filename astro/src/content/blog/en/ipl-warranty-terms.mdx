**Short answer:** an IPL warranty is a commercial term, and it is carried by four documents: the catalogue, the quotation, the proforma invoice and the manual. When those four disagree, the buyer argues from the earliest one, and the supplier usually loses the argument regardless of what the product actually deserved.

## The term is not one number

Warranty length varies by platform, and the reason is usually component quality rather than marketing. A device with a different lamp, a different cooling design or a different adapter carries a different exposure, so the term follows the build.

That is defensible. What is not defensible is a term that changes without the change being stated.

Two rules keep it clean:

- **State the term per model, in writing, on every document.** Not "12 to 24 months" and not a blank field for later.
- **If the term differs from what a customer was told, say so explicitly and say why.** A shorter term disclosed is a negotiation. A shorter term discovered is a broken account.

## Four documents, one term

| Document | What it should state | What happens when it is vague |
|---|---|---|
| Catalogue or spec sheet | The term per platform | The buyer quotes it to their own customers |
| Quotation | The term, next to the price and the MOQ | Price is negotiated, term is assumed |
| Proforma invoice | The term, as a line item | No contractual hook, so disputes become goodwill |
| User manual | The term and the exclusion list, in the market's language | The end customer reads a different story from the buyer |

The proforma invoice row is where this most often fails. A PI that lists product, quantity, price, Incoterm and payment terms but omits warranty reads as if the warranty were not part of the deal. When a claim arrives eighteen months later, the buyer produces the quotation that mentioned a term, and the supplier produces a PI that did not.

## What "covered" has to mean

A warranty without a stated scope is a promise to argue. Four things belong in the scope:

**The defect class.** Material and workmanship defects under normal use. That phrasing is standard, and it is specific enough to exclude design changes the buyer asked for.

**The exclusion list.** Misuse, unauthorised repair, water ingress beyond the stated rating, use outside the rated supply, and force majeure. Each exclusion should be one line, and each should be checkable from the return.

**Who pays freight.** A warranty that covers the part but not the return freight is half a warranty, and it is the half buyers care about. State both directions: who pays to get the unit back, and who pays to send the replacement.

**What remedy applies.** Repair, replace the unit, or replace the part. Those are different costs and different lead times, and a supplier who leaves it open will always choose the cheapest, which may not be what the buyer expected.

## Spare parts decide the second year

The warranty term is the first half of the conversation. The second half is what happens after it expires.

For an IPL device, the parts that matter are the lamp cartridge, the adapter, the cooling assembly and the housing set. A supplier who can send a lamp cartridge turns a two-year-old unit into a repair. A supplier who cannot turns it into scrap and a lost customer.

Ask three questions before the first order:

1. Which parts can be supplied separately, and at what lead time?
2. For how long after the last production run can those parts be supplied?
3. Can a whole-unit replacement be arranged for a unit that cannot be repaired in the field?

The second question is the one nobody asks and everybody needs. A brand that builds an installed base of ten thousand units will, in year three, need parts for a model the factory stopped making.

## Extensions are a price, not a promise

A longer warranty is available commercially, usually as a per-unit uplift quoted per project. That is a legitimate product: the factory is taking on more exposure and charging for it.

Two cautions. The extension has to name the same exclusion list as the base term, or it is an unlimited liability dressed as a premium. And the extension has to be priced against the expected claim rate, not against the sales conversation, which is why some platforms are more expensive to extend than others.

If a supplier offers a five-year warranty at no extra cost, the honest reading is that the base term was under-priced rather than that the extension is generous.

## The pattern that damages accounts

Here is a failure we have committed ourselves, described plainly because it is the most useful part of this article.

A quotation stated a two-year warranty. A later reply, after other terms had been renegotiated, moved to one year without flagging the change. The buyer had asked about the warranty explicitly and recorded the answer. The correction never came, and the discrepancy sat in the thread until they raised it.

From the supplier's side it looked like a term adjustment. From the buyer's side it looked like a supplier whose numbers move. Every subsequent correction in that thread, including the ones that were right, was read through that lens.

The rule that came out of it: whenever a term changes after a quotation has gone out, the change gets a sentence of its own, in the same message, before anything else is discussed. It costs one line and it protects everything else.

## What we do, and what we do not

We state the term per platform on the quotation and again on the proforma invoice, include the exclusion list and the freight responsibility, list which parts can be supplied separately, and price any extension per project. We do not offer a warranty longer than the build supports, and we do not let a term change quietly between documents.
