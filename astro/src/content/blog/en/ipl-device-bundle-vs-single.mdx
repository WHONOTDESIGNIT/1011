# An IPL Handset Alone Is Half a Product. Here's What the Winning Brands Add to the Box

A brand owner asked us the other day whether to sell the IPL device on its own, or pair it with everything a customer needs to actually use it. It is a question we hear constantly, because we manufacture the device and the brand has to decide what goes in the box. The short answer is the second one. The long answer is that nearly every brand that wins in this category has already answered the question, and their answer shows up in what they ship.

We make IPL devices for brands, so we keep a close eye on how the people who sell them choose to sell them. What follows is what we found when we looked at RoseSkinCo, Nood, Braun, Philips, Ulike, Sensica, SmoothSkin, and Silk'n. Not their marketing copy. Their box contents.

## Why the device is never the whole product

IPL has a built-in sequence. You shave first, then treat, then calm the skin. The device only handles the middle step. Most brands also tell their customers to use the device once a week for around twelve weeks before moving to maintenance, so this is a sequence that repeats many times, not a one-off.

Shaving matters for a specific reason. The light has to reach the hair root, and a long hair above the skin will absorb the energy and burn instead of letting it travel down the follicle. Dermatologists give the same instruction for any light-based hair removal, and the [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/cosmetic/hair-removal/laser-hair-removal-faqs) lists shaving as the prep step. Every IPL brand instructs its customers to shave before treating. That single instruction is what makes the razor the natural first companion in any IPL kit.

Once you see the sequence, the bundle strategy falls out of it. The brands that understand the routine build the box around the routine. The brands that don't sell a gadget and leave the customer to source the prep and the aftercare somewhere else.

## RoseSkinCo and Nood sell the routine, not the machine

RoseSkinCo built its business on the Lumi handset, but look at what they actually push. Their Silky Smooth+ Routine bundles the Lumi with a 4D shaver, a pair of exfoliating gloves, and a body lotion. That is the full before, during, and after sequence in one box. The device is the anchor, but the routine is the product.

Nood goes further. Their Flasher Pro kit ships the device with a five-blade razor, an exfoliating bar, a moisturizing shave oil, and a post-shave serum. Five items in the box, and four of them are not the IPL machine. The razor and the exfoliating bar handle the prep. The serum handles the after. Nood understood that if you give the customer every step, you also capture the revenue for every step instead of letting them walk to a drugstore for the razor.

There is a second reason this works, and it is about trust. A customer who opens a box and finds the razor, the prep, and the aftercare all together feels like someone thought through their actual week. That feeling is worth more than any spec sheet. The lesson is not that you should copy the exact items. It is that a bundle is a claim about how the product fits into a life, and a single handset makes no such claim.

## Braun and Philips lean on the razor and the precision head

The big electronics brands arrive at the same place by a different route. Braun sells the Silk Expert Pro 5 in several configurations, and the differences are all accessories. One SKU ships with a Venus razor. Another ships with a precision head, a Venus razor, and a premium pouch. A third ships with a precision head, a Venus razor, and a soft pouch. Same device in every case. The tiering is done entirely by what rides along.

Braun owns the Venus razor brand, so the razor in the box is not a third-party add-on. It is a second Braun product riding along, and every IPL customer who gets one has effectively been introduced to the Venus line. That is cross-selling baked into the unboxing.

Philips does the same with precision. The Lumea 9900 series ships with interchangeable attachments for the body, the face, the bikini line, and the underarms, plus an app for scheduling sessions. We took a Lumea apart and wrote about what we found in [the teardown](/blog/beating-the-philips-lumea), if you want the engineering view. Philips does not sell one window that does everything. It sells several heads and makes each one a reason the device costs what it costs. The pattern is consistent across both companies. The handset is the platform, and the accessories are the justification.

## Ulike sells the case and the collaboration

Ulike is the interesting case because it bundles status, not utility. The Air 10 ships with an optional leather case, and Ulike has run a Hello Kitty limited edition. The leather case does not change how the device performs. It changes how the device feels to own.

That matters more than it sounds. A customer who buys a leather case has made a second decision to invest in the product, and every decision to invest makes the next one easier. The collaboration does something similar. It borrows affection from a character the buyer already loves, and that borrowed affection becomes part of the price.

The carrying case also answers a real, private problem. Hair removal is intimate, and a device that gets used near the body wants to stay clean and out of sight. A case turns a bare tool into something you can store in a bathroom drawer without it touching everything else in there. There are three common tiers of this. A soft pouch is the cheapest and covers the portability need. A hard case adds protection for travel. A leather case, the Ulike approach, adds the status layer on top. Each tier is a different answer to the same two questions. Where does this live, and what does owning it say about me.

Here is a real number to put this in perspective. Remozer, a Japanese Amazon brand, ships its devices with a carrying case. The case carries a minimum order of 300 units, it takes a gold-foil logo printed on the surface, and it costs 8.95 USD per unit at that quantity. Order 500 at once and the price drops to 4.95 USD. A premium-looking, logoed accessory for under five dollars at scale. That is the gap between what a case costs a brand and what it is worth to a customer, and it is the clearest reason the case belongs in every IPL box.

## Sensica, SmoothSkin, and Silk'n each pick one thing

Sensica sells a Silky Summer Skin Duo that pairs the Sensilight with a second treatment product, leaning on the idea of a summer routine rather than a gadget. The pairing is seasonal, which tells you something about how they think. The bundle is not permanent hardware. It is a moment.

SmoothSkin makes the precision head the point. The Pure Fit ships with a precision head for small areas, which is their answer to the same problem Philips solves with multiple attachments. One company multiplies heads, the other adds one well-chosen head. Both are correct. The question is only whether your brand wants to look abundant or focused.

Silk'n has historically leaned on the cartridge. The Infinity line is rated for a huge number of flashes, and the replacement cartridge is the long-term companion to the initial purchase. The cartridge is a different kind of bundle. It is the accessory you buy later, not the one you buy first, but it locks the customer into the platform the same way. A customer who has bought a cartridge has no reason to switch brands, because the next cartridge fits the machine they already own.

Each of these is a single decision made consistently. That consistency is what a bundle actually is. It is not a pile of items. It is a point of view repeated across every box.

## What the data in the box tells us

Step back and the eight brands sort into three camps.

The routine camp. RoseSkinCo and Nood bundle the full before, during, after sequence. Razor, prep, device, aftercare. This is the highest-ticket approach and the one that captures the most revenue per customer. It also fits a skincare-adjacent brand, because skincare customers already think in routines.

The platform camp. Braun and Philips bundle accessories that make the handset itself feel more complete. A razor here, a precision head there, a pouch to hold it all. The device stays the hero. This fits a retail brand competing on shelf, where visible accessories read as value.

The status camp. Ulike bundles ownership signals. A leather case, a limited edition. Nothing about performance, everything about how it feels to own. This fits a brand chasing a younger, image-driven buyer.

None of them sell a bare handset as the flagship offer. Some sell a bare handset as the entry SKU, but the money and the brand story live in the kit. There is a reason for that, and it is simple economics. The device has a fixed cost and a fixed margin. A razor and a pouch cost very little to source but add perceived value out of proportion to their cost. A customer pays more for the kit, feels they got more, and the brand keeps more of the margin. The bundle is how you raise the order value without raising the device price.

## The psychology and the economics underneath

The three camps differ in what they put in the box, but they all lean on the same handful of principles.

Anchoring comes first. A bare handset sets a price in the customer's head. The kit then looks generous by comparison, even when the kit is the product the brand actually wanted to sell. The bare unit is rarely the goal. It is the number the customer measures everything else against.

Then there is commitment. Every extra decision to invest makes the next one easier. A customer who adds a case, or a razor, or a serum has said yes more than once, and each yes makes the relationship stickier. This is why the second accessory often matters more than the first one for retention.

Margin is the business side of the same coin. The device carries a fixed cost and a fixed margin. A razor, a pouch, and a case cost very little to source and add perceived value out of proportion to their cost. The Remozer number above is the whole argument in one line. A case that costs the brand under five dollars can read as a twenty or thirty dollar accessory to the customer. That spread is where a bundle earns its keep.

Cross-selling and lock-in are the two long games. Braun puts a Venus razor in the box and introduces the customer to a second product line. Silk'n sells a replacement cartridge and makes the next purchase automatic. Both are ways of making the first sale the start of a series instead of the end of one.

Finally there is loss aversion, the quiet engine of the whole thing. A kit priced as a bundle with a stated saving makes the customer feel they are losing money if they buy the device alone. The psychology is not new, and it is well documented. [Harvard Business School's research on pricing psychology](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/use-the-psychology-of-pricing-to-keep-customers-returning) and [Forbes' coverage of bundling](https://www.forbes.com/sites/hbsworkingknowledge/2013/01/18/product-bundling-is-a-smart-strategy-but-theres-a-catch/) point the same way. It is the same reason a fast-food combo works. But it is applied here to a three-hundred-dollar device, where the stakes and the margin are both higher.

## What a brand owner should actually do

If you are launching an IPL line, start from the routine, not the device.

First, decide which camp you are in. A DTC brand with a skincare angle should probably build a routine kit like RoseSkinCo or Nood, because your customer already thinks in routines. A retail brand competing on shelf should probably follow Braun or Philips, because precision heads and a branded razor read as value on a shelf. A brand chasing a younger, image-driven buyer should look at what Ulike does with the case and the collaboration. Pick one camp and commit to it across every SKU. A brand that tries to be all three ends up selling a pile of items instead of a point of view.

Second, at minimum, put the razor in the box. It is the one accessory the customer will definitely need, it is cheap to source, and it turns a single purchase into a complete one. A customer who gets the razor in the box does not have a reason to postpone the first session. A customer who does not get it has to go buy one, and every extra step between purchase and first use is a step where the return becomes more likely.

Third, give the device somewhere clean to live. A pouch or a case is not just packaging. It is the answer to a real privacy and hygiene need, and it is the cheapest way to make the product feel considered. If you want to push further, a leather or hard case is the status layer, and it is the accessory that costs the least to add but does the most for the unboxing.

We build the device side of this for brands, and the honest version is that the device is the easy part. These handsets are Class II medical devices in the US, and the clearance is [searchable in the FDA database](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPMN/pmn.cfm), so the box contents are the last thing a customer has to take on faith. The hard part is deciding what story the box tells. The brands that win are the ones that decided, and then put that decision in the box, every time. The handset is the engine. The box is the product.

A practical note for anyone sourcing this way. The razor, the pouch, and the case are not things you need to invent, and they are not things you need to buy from three separate suppliers. They are commodity items a manufacturer can quote alongside the device, each carrying your logo, all shipping from one place. That is what keeps a bundle profitable. The device carries the cost of the platform, the accessories carry the margin, and sourcing them together is where the numbers actually work.

One more thing. Many brands keep a bare handset as a low entry SKU and put the real margin in the kit. That is a sound structure. The bare unit sets the price anchor and the kit looks generous by comparison. What matters is that the kit exists. A brand with only a bare unit has already given up the highest-margin sale in the category.
