**Short answer:** an IPL hair removal device supplied in Australia is a medical device. It needs an ARTG entry before the first unit is sold, the application is filed by an Australian sponsor rather than the factory, and the evidence that gets it through comes from the factory. Australia is usually the slowest of the major markets to enter, and the sponsor relationship is the reason.

## The classification question comes first, and it is not optional

TGA treats intense pulsed light devices for hair removal as medical devices, at a class that requires conformity assessment evidence rather than a self-declaration. In practice that means Class IIa.

The class matters because it decides what the sponsor can file. A device that sits below the assessed classes can go in on a manufacturer's declaration. IPL does not.

Confirm the classification for your specific device with the sponsor before you build a file around it. Classification follows intended purpose and claims, and a listing that promises follicle-level permanent reduction reads differently from one positioned as a grooming appliance. In the EU that difference changes the route. In Australia it can change the class.

## The sponsor is the party with legal skin in the game

An overseas manufacturer cannot hold an ARTG entry. The entry belongs to an Australian sponsor, who becomes legally responsible for the device in Australia for as long as it is on the register.

That single sentence explains most of the friction, and four things follow from it:

- **The sponsor's name goes on the label**, alongside the manufacturer's. Not the brand's, unless the brand is the Australian entity.
- **The sponsor files and owns the application.** The factory supplies evidence; it does not apply.
- **Adverse events route to the sponsor first.** They have reporting obligations with deadlines that do not wait for a factory reply.
- **The sponsor carries the annual charge** for keeping the entry on the register, which is why sponsors structure commercial terms around volume.

A brand with no Australian entity therefore needs a sponsor before it needs anything else. Same structural position as the EU responsible person, with a heavier legal load.

## Four questions that decide the timeline

Every Australian project I have seen runs into the same four decision points. The order matters, because each one gates the next.

**One: which conformity assessment route will the sponsor use?** TGA accepts evidence produced in different ways. The three that come up most for IPL:

| Route | What the sponsor files | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| TGA conformity assessment | Application to TGA, with the full technical file, for TGA to issue the certificate | Slowest, most control, and TGA reviews to its own standard |
| MDSAP certificate | A current MDSAP certificate covering the manufacturing site and scope | Fastest if the factory already holds one for the right scope |
| European notified body certificate | A valid certificate from an EU notified body, with the technical documentation behind it | Depends entirely on whether the certificate scope matches the device |

The route is chosen by whoever holds the evidence. A factory with an MDSAP certificate covering the device scope shortens the project by months. A factory without one pushes the sponsor toward TGA assessment, which is the long path.

**Two: does the certificate scope actually cover this device?** This is where Australian projects die quietly. A certificate covering a manufacturing site for one product family does not automatically cover a new model, and the sponsor has no discretion to stretch it. Check the scope wording before the plan is built on it.

**Three: what performance and clinical evidence does the sponsor expect?** IPL is a device that makes a hair reduction claim, so the file needs evidence behind that claim, not just electrical safety. Test reports that prove the device emits what the specification says, and data that supports the stated reduction, both belong in the file. A safety-only pack gets returned.

**Four: what does the label and IFU have to say?** The sponsor's name and address, the Australian requirements for instructions, and the warnings that come with the intended purpose. The IFU is part of the regulated product, not a printing detail.

## What the sponsor asks the factory for

| Document | Detail | Where projects lose time |
|---|---|---|
| Technical file | Design, construction, component and software information | Written for CE and missing the performance rationale TGA expects |
| Test reports | Safety and EMC, to the standards named in the file | Report covers an earlier revision of the device |
| Performance evidence | Output measurements against the claimed specification | Claim in the IFU is broader than the data behind it |
| Risk management file | To ISO 14971, with the hazards tied to the actual design | Generic template, not traceable to this device |
| Labelling and IFU | Draft artwork including sponsor block | Drafted before the sponsor was identified |
| Manufacturing evidence | Site certificates, scope, and audit history | Certificate scope does not name the product family |
| Post-market data | Complaint and return records, if the device has been sold elsewhere | No records kept, so the sponsor has nothing to file |

The last row catches factories that have sold the device in other markets for years. If the complaint data was never kept in a form a sponsor can use, the Australian file starts from zero even though the product has history.

## Why Australia ends up last on the list

Three markets, three different bottlenecks. Put them next to each other and the reason Australia slips down the order becomes obvious.

| Market | What gates the launch | Who holds the responsibility |
|---|---|---|
| EU | Responsible person named on the product | The EU operator, appointed by the seller |
| Japan | PSE route and a Japanese importer | The importer, who files with METI |
| Australia | ARTG entry on a public register | The sponsor, legally responsible for the device |

The EU and Japan gates can be cleared with documents and an appointment. The Australian gate is a public register entry that depends on a third party's willingness to carry legal liability, and on evidence that has to be assessed. That is why brands that launch in Europe and Japan in the same quarter usually reach Australia a year later.
