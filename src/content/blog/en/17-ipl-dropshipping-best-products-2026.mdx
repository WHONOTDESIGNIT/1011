# In 2026, If You're Doing Dropshipping, I'd Point You to IPL Hair Removal Devices

Nine out of ten people starting dropshipping think of clothing and shoes first. The global apparel market is worth $1.7 trillion and is projected to reach $2 trillion by 2028 — a number that reassures anyone who sees it.

Big numbers, bigger pitfalls.

Clothing forces you to carry the full weight of sizes, colors, returns, and an endless queue of customer service questions. Customers ask "I'm 5'4", 140 lbs, what size should I buy?" — you can only guess, and when you guess wrong, it's a return, and you pay the return shipping. Roll that across dozens of SKUs and the margin is eaten away fast.

There's a more comfortable category. IPL hair removal devices sit at the intersection of three directions: beauty tech, high perceived value, and a per-unit math that's unusually dropshipping-friendly. Let me lay out the ledger — why it beats clothing, how to validate demand with third-party ad tools, and what the factory-side cost structure looks like.

## Why clothing is a dropshipping trap

Shopify's official dropshipping product guide recommends clothing and shoes as its first category. The reason is direct: the market is huge — $1.7 trillion, growing into 2028.

What the guide misses is something lethal: SKU explosion.

One t-shirt in 5 sizes and 4 colors is 20 SKUs. Add seasonal styles, men's and women's cuts, different fabrics, and you've piled up hundreds of SKUs. Every return has to be checked for size, color, condition, and whether it can be relisted.

Customers chase you for size advice. When you recommend wrong, they hold it against you. Online apparel return rates regularly exceed 20% to 30%. In dropshipping, you don't even get to inspect a return before it ships back to the supplier — you pay the return shipping, and the supplier may not even refund. At that point you can cross "business" off the list; what remains is a low-margin logistics grind.

## The beauty market is half the size of apparel — and so is the competition

Shopify's guide spells it out: the beauty industry — skincare, makeup, haircare, fragrance, and personal care combined — was valued at over $758 billion in 2025.

That's only about half the size of the apparel market, yet far fewer people do dropshipping in high-ticket hardware categories. Most crowd into $15 pimple patches and $22 serums, and steer around hardware, thinking it's too complicated. They're wrong.

The guide names two high-growth subcategories: natural skincare, growing 6.3% a year, and problem-solving haircare products. See the pattern? These categories all solve a specific problem. Pimple patches deal with acne, hair growth serums deal with thinning hair — and IPL hair removal devices deal with unwanted body hair, a problem 80% of adults manage in some form.

## The OYO example: an intersection of two trends

The guide also features OYO Skin Care, a brand founded by two Swedish sisters. They took off by placing their products between two categories — natural skincare, which sells trust, ingredients, and health, and problem-solving haircare, which sells specific, measurable results.

IPL hair removal devices sit at an even cleverer spot.

| Three directions | Why they matter |
|---|---|
| Beauty tech | High perceived value, price-insensitive |
| Problem-solving | Reduced hair growth is a measurable outcome that builds repeat customers |
| Home device | One purchase, ongoing accessory repurchases |

A bottle of serum is gone in 60 days. An IPL hair removal device comes home once, and afterward replacement heads, goggles, storage pouches, and accessories keep coming back to you. Once a customer trusts your device, the business becomes one purchase plus continuous repurchase, and customer lifetime value jumps.

## Three hard truths about why IPL devices suit dropshipping

### 60% to 75% margin — here's the math

Dropshipping clothing, after returns and ads, generally lands at 10% to 30% margin. IPL is a completely different number.

Sourcing from the origin factory — one like iShine that holds FDA clearance and MDSAP certification — the landed cost is about $60, worldwide shipping included. That $60 covers a lot: custom logos and labels on the device and packaging, a retail box designed to your spec, an English instruction manual, and the full set of FDA, CE, and ISO 13485 safety certifications.

The suggested retail price: $199 to $250.

| Price | Margin | Margin rate |
|---|---|---|
| $199 | $139 | 70% |
| $250 | $190 | 76% |

Compare with clothing. A $39 t-shirt landing at $15 gives a $24 margin, 61% — looks decent — but factor in a 25% return rate and the real margin drops below 45%. An IPL hair removal device, with marketing done right, can hold returns under 5%.

That 70%+ margin is your ammunition for Meta ads. When a product earns $140 per order, you can afford a $50 customer acquisition cost.

### No sizes, so customer service stays quiet

Open the clothing section of any dropshipping forum and the complaints look identical every day. Customer asks for a size recommendation, you give one, it doesn't fit, and a one-star review follows.

IPL hair removal devices have no sizes. No "does this run small?", no "I'm torn between two sizes."

The questions customers do ask basically fall into three types:

- Can dark skin use it — see the Fitzpatrick skin tone scale in the manual
- Can blondes and redheads use it — results are poor; IPL needs melanin to work, and this has to be stated in black and white on the product page
- How long until I see results — 8 to 12 weeks of consistent use

All standard answers. Build the FAQ page once and it automatically deflects 90% of customer service questions. Apparel customer service never ends. Shipping times also need to be stated up front. The US Federal Trade Commission's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule, [16 CFR Part 435](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule), enacted back in 1975, requires sellers to ship within the promised time; if no time is stated on the page, ship within 30 days, and if you can't, you must get buyer consent or refund.

### No dominant brand in the category yet

Ask five people what jeans brands exist — Levi's, Zara, H&M, Uniqlo come out instantly. Apparel brands are saturated.

Ask five people what IPL hair removal device brands exist — some can name Philips, Braun, Ulike; most can't think of a single name. The category has no dominant brand.

With no dominant brand, your store has a chance to become the name in its niche. Narrow to one country, say Germany. Or serve one skin tone, say Fitzpatrick IV to V. Or lead with a single selling point: FDA-cleared affordable IPL. Owning one niche is enough to live on.

What gates this industry is trust and evidence — and that's exactly what ad intelligence tools solve.

## Before spending a cent on ads, see where others are spending

Beginners pick products by guessing. It looks "cool," or a friend recommended it, and they go. Picking products by guessing is gambling with money.

The smarter move is watching where other people are already spending. Tools like BigSpy and AdSpy scrape millions of ads from Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. You can search by keyword — say, IPL hair removal — or type in a domain directly, like roseskinco.com, and limit by time range: last 30 days, 90 days, or a year.

A product that's run ads continuously for 6+ months, still spending, with hundreds of creatives updated frequently — it's basically making money. Think about it: who keeps pouring money into ads for half a year on a business that doesn't profit?

BigSpy is enough, and it has a free tier. Find 5 to 10 IPL brands that keep advertising, study their creative angles — pain points, before-and-after, social proof — look at pricing and offers, and estimate their spend scale; the count of active creatives is a ready reference.

Then compete on their weakest dimension: price, shipping speed, skin tone coverage, or bundle packages.

### What to read in ad data

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Same ad running 90+ days | The creative has survived validation. Don't copy it — understand the hook |
| Multiple ad accounts running at once | The brand is scaling budget; ROI is positive |
| Very frequent creative updates | Continuously optimizing — they value this product |
| Seasonal patterns | Demand peaks before summer — bikini season — and right after New Year's resolutions |

Don't copy their ads outright — copyright issues get accounts banned directly. Learn the structure. Take one ad apart: the first 3 seconds, pain point statement, solution reveal, social proof, call to action — what each of the five segments does, and you understand it after one pass.

A 68% margin means customer acquisition cost breaks even at $127. On Meta, beauty category acquisition costs generally run $30 to $60 — that gives you a two-times safety cushion.

## How to hold returns under 5%

IPL hair removal devices are naturally low-return — provided two things are done right.

Put a skin tone compatibility table on the product page, stating clearly that Fitzpatrick V to VI skin is not suitable and types I to IV are usable. Include a patch test instruction in the box, requiring customers to test a small area of skin before regular use.

Do those two things and returns hold steady under 5%. Apparel runs 20% to 30% — that single difference alone saves thousands of dollars a year in reverse logistics.

## How to scale: Meta ads plus ABM plus user content

You hold a product with a 68% margin; next comes traffic. This playbook runs in four steps.

1. Import the first 500 customer emails into Meta and build a custom audience — even one gathered from a small-scale launch counts.
2. Build a 1% lookalike audience from that seed group.
3. Run one video ad showing real device use, using real customers' before-and-after footage.
4. Retarget the 75% who watched the video with a 10% discount or free accessories.

This playbook doesn't rely on guessing — successful IPL sellers interviewed by Shopify Masters all use these four steps.

There's one more channel most people ignore: selling devices to influencers and small merchants, who then sell to their own followers. Same ABM approach — marketing around target customers — except the "customers" here are specific accounts rather than companies. Target beauty-focused Instagram accounts with 10,000 to 50,000 followers — engaged, and not expensive. Send a free device in exchange for an honest review video. Give them an affiliate link with a 15% to 20% commission on sales, and let them sell for you.

You become the upstream supplier to micro-influencers. They do the marketing, you handle fulfillment — each earning their own way.
