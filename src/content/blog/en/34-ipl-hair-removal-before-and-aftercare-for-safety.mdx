People who search "IPL before and aftercare" usually carry a fear, and the fears are typically these few.

- Burns and blistering
- Redness that won't fade for a long time
- Dark marks left behind (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation)
- Getting more sensitive with each session

In the years we've spent making devices, the safety problems we've seen basically come from two sources. One: flashing light onto skin that's already inflamed or highly sensitive. Two: piling more irritation on after the session — sun, heat, friction, and irritating skincare all count. What aftercare does is simple: hold the skin barrier steady and block the inflammation that can be avoided. Hold those two lines and most of the trouble never shows up.

## Before Use

**Step one: shave — don't wax, don't tweeze.** Shave the hair clean before use. Shaving only removes the hair above the surface; the root stays in the follicle, which is what the light needs to work on. Waxing and tweezing pull the root out too, so during a course of treatment the follicles are often empty — results suffer, and the skin gets more irritated. It looks like a small step; don't skip it. The whole sequence is three steps: shave first, confirm the skin is clean and dry, and don't apply oils or thick creams right before flashing.

**Step two: avoid sun — this is the easiest one to prevent yourself.** If you've been in the sun recently, don't rush the session. Sun-exposed skin has more epidermal melanin, and the same beam of light gets absorbed more, so burn and pigmentation risk climb with it. The execution is simple: don't treat sunburned skin that hasn't healed, and don't treat skin that has just picked up color either. Hold this line and you've removed the single most likely source of trouble in advance.

This risk has been measured in a controlled trial specifically. In 2013, *Lasers in Surgery and Medicine* published a randomized controlled trial comparing adverse reactions between natural skin and UV-tanned skin under the same low-energy IPL — check the original if you want to verify: [the study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24037900/).

**Step three: keep skincare minimal.** Keep the skincare on the treatment area under control — no strong irritants on it, and if your skin is prone to sensitivity, cut the routine down to the minimum. Skincare problems mostly come from stacking irritation, and the common stacks are these.

- Exfoliating acids and flashing on the same day
- Using retinoid products with no buffer period in between
- Applying heavily fragranced products right before or after treatment

## After Use

**Step one: cool down and protect the barrier.** After the session, cool the skin first while protecting the skin barrier. Gentle cooling plus moisturizing presses the redness down. Cooling doesn't need to chase ice — a cool towel for a short while is enough, then apply a simple moisturizer with minimal ingredients. For the rest of the day, don't touch that area with fragranced active products.

**Step two: for the window right after treatment, avoid heat and friction.** Saunas, hot showers, tight clothing — do them right after the session and inflammation gets amplified, and pigment problems are exactly what inflammation brings. In daily life, that translates to these rules.

- Keep shower water lukewarm
- Skip workouts that rub the treatment area repeatedly
- If your skin reacts easily, don't pull tight pants over freshly treated legs

**Step three: sun protection.** UV will worsen redness and pigment changes. The parts that will be exposed after treatment matter most — arms, legs, and the facial areas the instructions permit — all need coverage.

When choosing sunscreen, look for two things: broad spectrum and SPF 30 or higher. That's the standard the American Academy of Dermatology recommends: [how to choose the right sunscreen](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/choosing-right-sunscreen).

## When to Continue, When to Stop

The judgment standard isn't hard to remember. First, what counts as normal.

- Mild redness that fades within a few hours
- Mild warmth or stinging that disappears quickly
- Goosebump-like dots around follicles that settle within a day or two

These mean you can continue as planned. The following mean stop and reassess.

- A burning pain during flashing
- Redness that keeps getting worse within hours
- Blisters or crusting
- Color that keeps deepening over several days

That last one deserves the closest watch — it may be the start of pigment change. For a fuller normal-versus-abnormal comparison, see [IPL side effects in detail (what's normal and what isn't)](/blog/32-ipl-hair-removal-side-effects-what-is-normal).

## A Word for Brands

The last section is for friends building brands. Care instructions are part of the product experience — don't treat them as a disclaimer to brush off. Brands that do this well generally handle it like this.

- Make the care guidance a short checklist
- Put the stop signals in bold, in a visible spot — not buried in a corner
- Link to the contraindications article and the operating protocol

And one sentence worth repeating to users: using it more often doesn't mean more effective — when irritation goes up, results don't double with it. Write the care instructions clearly, and you'll answer fewer after-sales calls.
